Random selection of a chord

A chord is selected at random in a circle. What is the probability that it is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle?

We will calculate three answers to this same question, then make the probability models precise.

Pose the question before specifying a probability triplet. Let the three calculations motivate the formal models.

Reference: Jagannathan, Chapter 4; rigorous supplement §3 for the sampling maps and derivations.

The chord-length question

For a circle of radius RR, find the probability that a sampled chord has length L>3RL>\sqrt3R. What probability would you assign?

The reference length is 3R\sqrt3R.

We will calculate the probability under three specified sampling rules.

In each case the required event is the same geometric condition:

L>3R.L>\sqrt3R.

Chord length from the midpoint distance

Let D be the distance from the centre to the chord. Express its length L in terms of D.

The right triangle gives (L/2)2+D2=R2(L/2)^2+D^2=R^2.

Therefore L=2R2−D2L=2\sqrt{R^2-D^2}.

Converting the long-chord condition

Using L=2R2−D2L=2\sqrt{R^2-D^2}, express the strict condition L>3RL>\sqrt3R as an inequality for DD.

The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, giving

(L/2)2+D2=R2,L=2R2−D2.(L/2)^2+D^2=R^2,\qquad L=2\sqrt{R^2-D^2}.

Now apply the strict threshold:

2R2−D2>3R.2\sqrt{R^2-D^2}>\sqrt3R.

Both sides are nonnegative, so squaring is valid:

4(R2−D2)>3R2⇔D2<R2/4.4(R^2-D^2)>3R^2\iff D^2<R^2/4.

Since D≥0D\ge0, the event is D<R/2D<R/2.

Experiment I: a uniform angle at a fixed endpoint

Use a unit circle and fix a=(1,0)a=(1,0). Choose θ\theta uniformly in (0,π)(0,\pi), measured from the upward tangent towards the disk. Find the long-chord probability.

The resulting chord has length L=2sin⁡θL=2\sin\,\theta.

L>3⇔π/3<θ<2π/3.L>\sqrt3\iff\pi/3<\theta<2\pi/3.

The favorable interval occupies one third of the angular range:

2π/3−π/3π=13.\frac{2\pi/3-\pi/3}{\pi}=\frac13.

Experiment II: a uniform distance

Choose rr uniformly in (0,1)(0,1). Set the midpoint to (r,0)(r,0) and draw the vertical chord through it. Find the long-chord probability.

L=21−r2,L>3⇔r<1/2.L=2\sqrt{1-r^2},\qquad L>\sqrt3\iff r<1/2.

Half the available distance interval is favorable:

1/21=12.\frac{1/2}{1}=\frac12.

Fixing the midpoint direction specifies which chord is drawn.

Experiment III: a uniform midpoint

Choose a midpoint uniformly by area in the unit disk. Draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point. Find the long-chord probability.

The midpoint must lie inside the concentric disk of radius 1/21/2.

π(1/2)2π=14.\frac{\pi(1/2)^2}{\pi}=\frac14.

The centre and boundary have area zero; omit them to make the construction unique and nondegenerate.

The probability triplets resolve the contradiction

With the probability triplets specified, the apparent contradiction is resolved: the three answers belong to three different probability models.

P1(E1)=13,P2(E2)=12,P3(E3)=14.P_1(E_1)=\frac13,\qquad P_2(E_2)=\frac12,\qquad P_3(E_3)=\frac14.

Stopping point. The resolution is complete. The remaining slides study sampling maps and induced measures on a common chord space.

Why uniform area uses a square root

For a midpoint sampled uniformly by area in a radius-R disk and U uniform on (0,1), derive P(D<r) and the corresponding transformation of U.

P(D<r)=πr2πR2=(rR)2.P(D<r)=\frac{\pi r^2}{\pi R^2}=\left(\frac rR\right)^2.

The transformation D=RUD=R\sqrt U has exactly this property:

P(RU<r)=P(U<(r/R)2)=(r/R)2.P(R\sqrt U<r)=P\!\left(U<(r/R)^2\right)=(r/R)^2.

Using D=RUD=RU instead would give uniform radial distance.
