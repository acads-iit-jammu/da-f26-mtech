Purpose and pace

We use worked problems to introduce outcomes, sample spaces, events, probability measures and random variables. The examples begin with a support audit and extend to electrical engineering, IT operations, finance, accounting, agriculture and training. All numerical scenarios are hypothetical.

For each problem, identify what is selected or generated, which outcomes meet the condition, and how the sampling rule assigns their probabilities. After a calculation, state the idea it illustrates and the limits of its interpretation.

The main notes use finite counting, weighted sums and simple areas. They include historical interpretations, Kolmogorov’s triplet and his three axioms. Short definition boxes explain the event collection and the probability axioms. The supplement develops their proofs, Borel sets and measurability; those technical developments are not prerequisites for the main problems.

Worked examples include their calculations as part of the teaching. Checkpoints and the separate practice questions are for students to solve. Stop at the suggested checkpoints and continue in the next meeting as needed.

1. A ticket-routing audit

An IT service desk is checking whether an LLM routes incoming tickets to the appropriate department. The pilot uses three reference outcomes:

“Other” is the complement category for this two-destination question: the reviewer finds that neither Billing nor Account Support is required. Another team may handle such a ticket; “Other” does not mean that the ticket was unreviewed or its status is unknown. A ticket may require both Billing and Account Support, so the reference outcome can contain both labels.

2. Historical development and interpretations

Problems of chance helped drive the development of probability: Pascal and Fermat’s correspondence in 1654 examined how to reason about uncertain outcomes in games. The translated correspondence shows the role of concrete problems. Later interpretations emphasized equal-likelihood counting, repeated observations, belief and evidential support. These remain different ways of understanding an assignment of probability, rather than a sequence in which each simply replaced the previous one.

Kolmogorov’s 1933 axiomatic foundation supplied a common mathematical framework for probability calculations; see the historical introduction in Imperial College’s probability notes. It specifies the rules a probability model must obey. It does not, by itself, choose the appropriate sampling mechanism or probability assignment for an application. We first compare interpretations using the audit, then use Bertrand’s question to see why a model needs to be specified.

Probability has been interpreted in several ways. The accounts below answer different questions about what the number 4/64/6 might mean. None follows just from seeing six rows in a table.

Classical equal-likelihood

The classical rule counts favourable outcomes among equally likely cases. If one ticket ID is selected uniformly from the six IDs, four selections produce agreement, so

P(agreement)=46=23.P(\text{agreement})=\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}.

This calculation is valid for that selection experiment. It does not imply that future tickets are equally likely to be any of these six records, or that the six records represent future traffic. A finite list defines possible cases; the uniform selection rule supplies the equal weights.

The partition chosen for counting also matters. Group the tickets by the pair (reviewer outcome, model classification):

Reviewer outcome Model classification Ticket IDs Exact match?
Billing and Account Support Billing t1t_1 No
Billing only Billing t2,t5t_2,t_5 Yes
Account Support only Account Support t3,t6t_3,t_6 Yes
Other Account Support t4t_4 No

There are four occupied pair types in this audit. If an experiment selected one of these four types uniformly, two of the four would be exact matches, giving 2/42/4. If it selected one of the six ticket IDs uniformly, the pair types would instead have probabilities 1/6,2/6,2/6,1/61/6,2/6,2/6,1/6, giving 4/64/6. Thresholding two destination scores separately permits four model outputs: neither, Billing only, Account Support only, or both. Thus the full product contains 4×4=164\times4=16 reviewer/model pairs, of which four occur in this audit. Uniform selection among all 16 pairs would be a third experiment; its four matching pairs would give agreement probability 4/16=1/44/16=1/4. Equal weighting of pair types does not follow from grouping tickets that way. Counting requires a stated set of cases and a reason to treat those cases as equally likely.

Frequency

The observed agreement proportion is

p̂6=16∑i=16Ii=46,\widehat p_6=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6}I_i=\frac{4}{6},

where Ii=1I_i=1 when the model classification exactly matches the reviewer’s reference outcome for ticket tit_i, and Ii=0I_i=0 otherwise. A frequency interpretation connects a probability to the limiting relative frequency in a specified repeatable process. This audit records one finite set of six tickets; it does not establish that limit or show that future ticket mix and model performance will remain the same.

Repeatedly sampling the LLM for the same text under randomized decoding would define another process. Its output frequency would describe generated classifications, not necessarily agreement with reviewer decisions. The process and event must be stated before interpreting a frequency.

Subjective probability and Bayesian updating

A subjective interpretation treats probability as a person’s degree of belief given available information. An analyst might assess the chance that the model agrees with the review protocol on a future ticket, using this audit and other stated information. The six rows do not determine that assessment uniquely. Coherence constrains probability assignments; subjective does not mean arbitrary. Countable additivity is an additional condition in the Kolmogorov model and does not follow from every finite coherence argument.

Bayesian updating describes how an assessment changes when evidence is observed. If HH is the proposition that the next ticket’s model classification will agree with its reference outcome and DD is additional evidence, Bayes’ rule gives

P(H∣D)=P(D∣H)P(H)P(D),P(D)>0.P(H\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid H)P(H)}{P(D)},\qquad P(D)>0.

The formula does not supply the prior or likelihood; those come from a model and stated information. Conditional probability and Bayesian updating will be developed later.

Logical or evidential probability

A logical or evidential interpretation aims to quantify how strongly stated evidence supports a proposition. Here the evidence could include the six audit rows, the review protocol and assumptions about the future ticket population. A numerical answer requires a rule connecting that evidence and those assumptions to a degree of support. If the rule invokes indifference, it must specify which cases are treated equally and why. Counting ticket IDs and counting occupied label-pair types give different answers because they describe different selection rules. This identifies a problem with an unspecified indifference rule; it does not refute every logical account.

What the interpretations leave for a probability model

Classical probability emphasizes equal likelihood, frequency emphasizes repetition, subjective probability represents an assessment given information, and logical accounts specify evidential support. A propensity account would treat the configured randomized generation process as the chance-producing mechanism. These accounts differ in interpretation. A mathematical calculation also needs the possible outcomes, the event of interest and a probability assignment. The next example shows why the sampling procedure is part of that specification.

3. A second question: Bertrand’s random chord

Draw an equilateral triangle inside a circle. Consider the question:

If a chord is selected at random from the circle, what is the probability that its length exceeds the side length of the triangle?

The phrase “selected at random” does not specify how the chord is generated. Bertrand described different sampling procedures for chords, which give 1/31/3, 1/21/2 and 1/41/4 for the same length comparison. These values follow from different probability distributions induced by the respective sampling procedures; the problem statement alone does not say which procedure to use. John D. Norton’s University of Pittsburgh page presents the three sampling arguments and their values.

The procedures are: choose two independent uniform endpoints (1/31/3); choose a uniform radial distance and an independent uniform direction (1/21/2); or choose a midpoint uniformly in disk area (1/41/4). These are different models. We will not choose among them or derive the answers in this section. The example identifies information a probability question can leave unspecified: the outcomes and, especially, the sampling rule that determines their probabilities.

Kolmogorov’s set-based framework

Kolmogorov’s axiomatic framework gives probability a mathematical foundation in terms of sets and a measure. Its three objects are written

(Ω,ℱ,P).(\Omega,\mathcal F,P).

Here Ω\Omega is the sample space of possible outcomes, ℱ\mathcal F is the collection of events to which probabilities are assigned, and P:ℱ→[0,1]P:\mathcal F\to[0,1] is the probability measure. The measure obeys nonnegativity, normalization and countable additivity. We will understand these rules through the ticket audit. Section 5 explains the event-collection requirements in a definition box; their further development belongs to the supplement. The supplement returns to Bertrand’s question and specifies the sampling models in full.

4. Define the experiment and its events

To calculate a probability for the audit, specify an experiment:

Select one of the six ticket IDs uniformly at random.

The ticket records, reviewer decisions and model classifications are fixed. The selected ticket ID is the outcome that varies. Thus the sample space is

Ω={t1,t2,t3,t4,t5,t6}.\Omega=\{t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4,t_5,t_6\}.

The sample space is the set of possible outcomes; exactly one outcome occurs in a selection. An event is a set of outcomes satisfying a condition. It occurs when the selected outcome belongs to that set.

Define events from the reviewer’s reference classification:

B={t1,t2,t5},A={t1,t3,t6},B=\{t_1,t_2,t_5\},\qquad A=\{t_1,t_3,t_6\},

where BB is the event “Billing is required” and AA is the event “Account Support is required.” The event that neither is required is

O=(B∪A)c={t4}.O=(B\cup A)^c=\{t_4\}.

In this model, OO is precisely the “Other” reference outcome. It is disjoint from BB and AA. The ticket t1t_1 belongs to both BB and AA, because both destinations are required:

B∩A={t1},B∪A={t1,t2,t3,t5,t6}.B\cap A=\{t_1\},\qquad B\cup A=\{t_1,t_2,t_3,t_5,t_6\}.

For sets E,F⊆ΩE,F\subseteq\Omega:

E∩F={t∈Ω:t∈E and t∈F},E∪F={t∈Ω:t∈E or t∈F},Ec=Ω\E.\begin{aligned} E\cap F&=\{t\in\Omega:t\in E\text{ and }t\in F\},\\ E\cup F&=\{t\in\Omega:t\in E\text{ or }t\in F\},\\ E^c&=\Omega\setminus E. \end{aligned}

The “or” in a union is inclusive. The outcome t1t_1 is counted in both marginal events, so to count tickets requiring at least one named destination we use the disjoint pieces B\AB\setminus A, A\BA\setminus B and B∩AB\cap A:

B∪A=(B\A)∪(A\B)∪(B∩A).B\cup A=(B\setminus A)\cup(A\setminus B)\cup(B\cap A).

Under uniform selection, their probabilities are 2/62/6, 2/62/6 and 1/61/6. Therefore

P(B∪A)=26+26+16=56.P(B\cup A)=\frac{2}{6}+\frac{2}{6}+\frac{1}{6}=\frac{5}{6}.

Equivalently,

P(B∪A)=P(B)+P(A)−P(B∩A)=36+36−16=56.P(B\cup A)=P(B)+P(A)-P(B\cap A) =\frac{3}{6}+\frac{3}{6}-\frac{1}{6}=\frac{5}{6}.

The complement is OO, so P(O)=1/6P(O)=1/6. The reviewer outcomes form four possible destination sets: both, Billing only, Account Support only, or neither. “Other” refers only to the last of these in this example.

The exact-agreement event is C={t2,t3,t5,t6}C=\{t_2,t_3,t_5,t_6\}, so P(C)=4/6P(C)=4/6 under this same uniform-ID experiment. We will also represent agreement numerically.

5. From a calculation to the triplet and axioms

In the audit, selecting Billing-only, Account-only, both, or neither has probabilities 2/6,2/6,1/6,1/62/6,2/6,1/6,1/6. These four categories do not overlap and cover every ticket, so their probabilities add to 1. No category has a negative probability. These observations motivate the rules for a probability model.

The experiment is represented by the probability triplet

(Ω,ℱ,P).(\Omega,\mathcal F,P).

Object Ticket-audit model Meaning
Ω\Omega {t1,t2,t3,t4,t5,t6}\{t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4,t_5,t_6\} Possible outcomes: ticket IDs that may be selected
ℱ\mathcal F 2Ω2^\Omega Events: all subsets of the six ticket IDs
PP P(E)=|E|/6P(E)=|E|/6 for E⊆ΩE\subseteq\Omega Probability measure under uniform selection

An outcome such as t2t_2 is an element of Ω\Omega. An event such as BB is a subset of Ω\Omega. The measure PP takes an event as input and returns a number in [0,1][0,1]. Here the uniform rule assigns probability 1/61/6 to each singleton outcome.

Definition: power set — the collection of all subsets

For a sample space Ω\Omega, its power set is

2Ω=𝒫(Ω)={E:E⊆Ω}.2^\Omega=\mathcal P(\Omega)=\{E:E\subseteq\Omega\}.

It is a set whose members are themselves sets. For Ω={a,b,c}\Omega=\{a,b,c\}, the full list is

𝒫(Ω)={⌀,{a},{b},{c},{a,b},{a,c},{b,c},{a,b,c}}.\mathcal P(\Omega)=\{\varnothing,\{a\},\{b\},\{c\}, \{a,b\},\{a,c\},\{b,c\},\{a,b,c\}\}.

Here a∈Ωa\in\Omega, {a}⊆Ω\{a\}\subseteq\Omega, and {a}∈𝒫(Ω)\{a\}\in\mathcal P(\Omega) are different statements. The empty set is the impossible event; Ω\Omega is the certain event. If Ω\Omega has nn elements, its power set has 2n2^n members: each element can be included or excluded from a subset. The six-ticket space therefore has 64 possible events, not 64 outcomes.

Choosing all subsets as events is convenient for our finite models. It allows questions about individual tickets as well as groups. This choice says nothing about whether their probabilities are equal.

Suppose instead that a dashboard on {a,b,c}\{a,b,c\} reports only “one of a,ba,b” or “cc.” It can answer the event {a,b}\{a,b\} and its opposite {c}\{c\}, together with the certain and impossible events. It cannot distinguish aa from bb. This motivates specifying which collection of events the model uses.

Definition: sigma-algebra — a collection of events closed under logical combinations

A sigma-algebra ℱ\mathcal F on Ω\Omega is a collection of subsets of Ω\Omega satisfying:

  1. Ω∈ℱ\Omega\in\mathcal F: the certain event is available.
  2. If E∈ℱE\in\mathcal F, then Ec=Ω\E∈ℱE^c=\Omega\setminus E\in\mathcal F: if a question is available, “not that event” is available.
  3. If E1,E2,…∈ℱE_1,E_2,\ldots\in\mathcal F, then ⋃n=1∞En∈ℱ\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_n\in\mathcal F: “at least one occurs” is available for a countable list of events.

Closed means that performing the stated operation keeps us inside the same collection. Countable means finite or listable as E1,E2,…E_1,E_2,\ldots; it does not mean an arbitrary collection of any size. The events in a union need not be disjoint for the union to belong to ℱ\mathcal F.

These requirements concern which sets are events, not the numerical probabilities assigned to them. In the triplet (Ω,ℱ,P)(\Omega,\mathcal F,P), ℱ\mathcal F supplies the events and PP assigns their probabilities.

Worked check: a full collection and a smaller collection

Every power set 𝒫(Ω)\mathcal P(\Omega) is a sigma-algebra: the whole space, complements and unions of subsets of Ω\Omega are still subsets of Ω\Omega.

For the dashboard, 𝒢={⌀,{a,b},{c},Ω}\mathcal G=\{\varnothing,\{a,b\},\{c\},\Omega\} is also a sigma-algebra. Complements exchange the two nontrivial groups, and every union is again one of the four listed sets. In particular, a countable union still combines only these four distinct sets. The set {a}\{a\} is a subset of Ω\Omega but is not an event in this smaller model.

By contrast, {⌀,Ω,{a}}\{\varnothing,\Omega,\{a\}\} is not a sigma-algebra on {a,b,c}\{a,b,c\}: it omits the required complement {b,c}\{b,c\}.

Useful properties of a sigma-algebra

The three requirements imply:

  • ⌀∈ℱ\varnothing\in\mathcal F, since ⌀=Ωc\varnothing=\Omega^c.
  • Countable intersections are events: “all occur” can be written as “not at least one fails,” ⋂nEn=(⋃nEnc)c.\bigcap_n E_n=\left(\bigcup_n E_n^c\right)^c.
  • Differences are events: E\F=E∩FcE\setminus F=E\cap F^c means “EE occurs and FF does not.”
  • Finite unions and intersections are included as special cases.

A sigma-algebra need not be the full power set. In continuous models we use an appropriate event collection containing intervals and their usual combinations; the supplement explains Borel sets and why further care is needed.

Kolmogorov's axioms — rules for the probability assignment

On the event collection ℱ\mathcal F, the probability measure PP satisfies:

  1. Nonnegativity: P(E)≥0P(E)\ge0 for every event EE.
  2. Normalization: P(Ω)=1P(\Omega)=1.
  3. Countable additivity: for pairwise disjoint events E1,E2,…E_1,E_2,\ldots, P(⋃n=1∞En)=∑n=1∞P(En).P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(E_n).

Pairwise disjoint means Ei∩Ej=⌀E_i\cap E_j=\varnothing whenever i≠ji\ne j. In this case there is no overlap to count twice. Unlike closure of a sigma-algebra under unions, adding probabilities without a correction requires disjointness.

The axioms do not say that outcomes are equally likely. Uniform and nonuniform assignments can both satisfy them.

For the six singleton events, additivity gives P(Ω)=6(1/6)=1P(\Omega)=6(1/6)=1. Also BB and BcB^c are disjoint and exhaustive, and P(B)+P(Bc)=3/6+3/6=1P(B)+P(B^c)=3/6+3/6=1. The same addition rule applies to any countable list of disjoint events; on this six-outcome space, only finitely many can be nonempty.

A probability measure is a set function: it takes an event as input and returns a number between 0 and 1. The event BB is a set of tickets; P(B)=1/2P(B)=1/2 is a number. This keeps the sample point, event and probability value distinct.

6. Record a number: random variables and distributions

Suppose the audit spreadsheet needs a 1 when Billing is required and a 0 otherwise. Selecting a ticket uniformly produces the entries 1,1,0,0,1,01,1,0,0,1,0 on the six possible rows. Three rows give 1, so the probability of recording 1 is 3/6=1/23/6=1/2.

A random variable assigns a numerical value to each outcome. For this experiment define the Billing indicator

XB:Ω→ℝ,XB(t)={1,t∈B,0,t∉B.X_B:\Omega\to\mathbb R,\qquad X_B(t)=\begin{cases} 1,&t\in B,\\ 0,&t\notin B. \end{cases}

Its values are

XB(t1)=1,XB(t2)=1,XB(t3)=0,XB(t4)=0,XB(t5)=1,XB(t6)=0.X_B(t_1)=1,\;X_B(t_2)=1,\;X_B(t_3)=0,\;X_B(t_4)=0, \;X_B(t_5)=1,\;X_B(t_6)=0.

Write X:Ω→ℝX:\Omega\to\mathbb R for a real-valued random variable. Once the outcome is known, its value is fixed. Here the selection of a ticket is random; the rule for recording Billing is fixed.

The inputs distinguish two functions: XBX_B takes one ticket and returns 0 or 1; PP takes a set of tickets and returns its probability. Thus PP is called a set function. The term describes the type of input. Having a set as its domain does not make every function a set function. This is a set of numerical values, not an event in the ticket sample space.

P(XB=1)=P(XB−1({1}))=P(B)=36=12.P(X_B=1)=P\bigl(X_B^{-1}(\{1\})\bigr)=P(B)=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12.

7. A continuous measurement: probability as area

A stipulated sensor model has error ε\varepsilon uniformly distributed between −2-2 and 22 mm. What is the probability that the error is between −0.5-0.5 and 0.50.5 mm?

Uniformity assigns probabilities in proportion to interval length. The favorable interval has length 1 mm, while the full interval has length 4 mm. Therefore

P(−0.5≤ε≤0.5)=14.P(-0.5\le\varepsilon\le0.5)=\frac{1}{4}.

We describe this with a probability density f(e)=1/4f(e)=1/4 per mm on [−2,2][-2,2], and zero elsewhere. The rectangle above an interval has area equal to its probability. Its total area is 4(1/4)=14(1/4)=1. More generally, a density is nonnegative, has total area 1, and gives interval probabilities by

P(a≤X≤b)=∫abf(x)dx.P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.

A density height is not a probability. Under this continuous model, an exact point has probability zero, although a measured error must have some value. Recorded, rounded measurements describe intervals rather than infinitely precise points. Not every random variable has a density: the head count in Section 6 has discrete probabilities.

The sample space here is [−2,2][-2,2]; the events used in these calculations are intervals and combinations of intervals; the probability measure assigns their lengths divided by 4. The precise event collection is developed in the unit-interval example in the supplement. Uniform error is an assumption for teaching, not a consequence of a manufacturer’s tolerance limit. A more involved closure-time density is also worked out in the supplement.

8. Worked problems across domains

A hypothetical ledger has 30 distinct invoice IDs: 21 reconcile correctly, 5 have only a missing receipt, 3 have only an amount mismatch, and 1 has both issues. Select one ID uniformly. Find the probabilities of any issue and of an amount mismatch.

Worked example 12: Agriculture: plot selection and area selection

Worked example 13: Finance: a loss distribution from fixed records

9. Connection to AI evaluation and continuation

The probability 2/32/3 here concerns uniform selection from six audited ticket IDs. It is not automatically the model’s agreement probability on future traffic. That requires a defined target population, an appropriate sampling process and assumptions connecting this audit to future tickets. Changing the reviewer protocol may also change the event called “agreement.”

The same distinctions arise in AI evaluation. A test-set accuracy is an observed proportion. Interpreting it as a probability of correct classification for future cases requires additional assumptions. If model generation is stochastic, its repeated-output distribution is another probability model with a different outcome space. In each case, specify the outcomes, event and sampling or generation process before interpreting a probability.

A suitable first stopping point is the calculation of P(B∪A)=5/6P(B\cup A)=5/6 at the end of Section 4. Continue to the triplet and axioms once the class can identify the complement event OO. Use Sections 6–8 for another session of numerical recordings, distributions and applications. The technical measure-theoretic development remains in the supplement. Bertrand’s chord problem is posed here but not derived; the rigorous supplement specifies its sampling models and works through their parametrizations and probabilities.
