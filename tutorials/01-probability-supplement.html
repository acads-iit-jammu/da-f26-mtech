Main lecture notes · Sampling-map demonstration

This supplement gives the formal definitions, parametrizations and derivations supporting the main lecture. Its details are intended for supplementary study alongside the corresponding lecture topics. The notation agrees with the lecture notes: RR is the fixed circle radius, TT the normalized midpoint distance, and XX the chord length.

Reference: Krishna Jagannathan, Probability Foundations for Electrical Engineers, 9 July 2025, Chapters 1, 4 and 11, particularly Definitions 68–70 for measurable functions, random variables and their laws. The calculations of the sampling maps are written out below.

1. Outcomes, events and mutual exclusivity

A sample space is required to satisfy two conditions. It must be exhaustive, so that at least one of its elements occurs in any realization of the experiment, and its elements must be mutually exclusive, so that at most one of them occurs. Together these state that exactly one ω∈Ω\omega\in\Omega occurs each time the experiment is performed.

Mutual exclusivity of outcomes is a property of the singleton events:

{ω1}∩{ω2}=⌀whenever ω1≠ω2.\{\omega_1\}\cap\{\omega_2\}=\varnothing\quad\text{whenever }\omega_1\ne\omega_2 .

For countable Ω\Omega the singletons therefore form a countable disjoint partition, Ω=⁡ω∈Ω{ω}\Omega=\mathop{\dot\bigcup}_{\omega\in\Omega}\{\omega\}.

Outcomes are mutually exclusive; events need not be. For a single roll of a die, Ω={1,2,3,4,5,6}\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}, and the outcomes 22 and 44 cannot both occur. The events

A={2,4,6}(even),B={4,5,6}(greater than 3)A=\{2,4,6\}\ \text{(even)},\qquad B=\{4,5,6\}\ \text{(greater than 3)}

can both occur, since A∩B={4,6}≠⌀A\cap B=\{4,6\}\ne\varnothing.

Consequences for calculation

The two cases are handled by different rules, and the distinction above is what separates them.

Because singletons are disjoint, countable additivity applies directly to them, and for countable Ω\Omega

P(A)=∑ω∈AP({ω}).P(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}P(\{\omega\}).

For two events no such rule is available, since additivity requires disjointness. The correction term is the shared part:

P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B).P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).

Writing P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B) for the die events above would count the outcomes 44 and 66 twice.

Two errors that this distinction prevents

A list of events is not a sample space. For one roll of a die, the pair “even” and “greater than 3” is not a sample space: both can occur in a single roll, so the second condition fails. They are events in the finer space {1,…,6}\{1,\ldots,6\}.

A valid sample space need not have equally likely elements. In the two-toss example of Section 5.1 of the main notes, the list “a head at once”, “a tail then a head”, “two tails” is exhaustive and mutually exclusive, so it is a legitimate sample space. Its members carry probabilities 1/21/2, 1/41/4 and 1/41/4. Mutual exclusivity is a requirement on the sample space; it says nothing about the probabilities assigned to its elements, and counting the members is valid only when they happen to be equally probable.

The same reading in the Bertrand construction

Each performance of any of the three sampling procedures produces exactly one chord. The chords are therefore the elementary outcomes, they are mutually exclusive, and 𝒞*\mathcal C_* is the sample space. The three models agree on this sample space and differ only in the measure placed on it.

The event E={c:X(c)>3R}E=\{c:X(c)>\sqrt3R\} is not an outcome. It is a set containing uncountably many chords, and it is assigned a probability as a set.

This last point also marks the limit of the summation rule above. In a continuous model each individual outcome has probability zero, so

∑c∈EP({c})=0≠P(E),\sum_{c\in E}P(\{c\})=0\ne P(E),

and the probability of EE cannot be recovered by adding the probabilities of the outcomes it contains. Countable additivity is additivity over countably many disjoint events, and EE is not a countable union of its singletons. The rule P(A)=∑ω∈AP({ω})P(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}P(\{\omega\}) is confined to countable sample spaces for this reason.

2. Random variables, inverse images and probability laws

On a probability space (Ω,ℱ,P)(\Omega,\mathcal F,P), a real-valued random variable is a measurable function

X:Ω→ℝ.X:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb R.

Measurability means that X−1(B)∈ℱX^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal F for every Borel set B∈ℬ(ℝ)B\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R). The Borel σ-algebra is generated by open intervals and includes the intervals, singleton sets and their countable combinations used here. For a real-valued function, it is sufficient to check {ω:X(ω)≤x}∈ℱ\{\omega:X(\omega)\le x\}\in\mathcal F for every real xx.

The distinction between a random variable and a set function

A random variable is a function on outcomes, not a real-valued set function on events. It induces the following map between collections of sets:

X−1:ℬ(ℝ)→ℱ,B↦{ω:X(ω)∈B}.X^{-1}:\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\longrightarrow\mathcal F,\qquad B\longmapsto\{\omega:X(\omega)\in B\}.

The probability measure and the distribution of XX are real-valued set functions:

P:ℱ→[0,1],PX:ℬ(ℝ)→[0,1],P:\mathcal F\to[0,1],\qquad P_X:\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\to[0,1],

PX(B)=P(X−1(B))=P(X∈B).\boxed{P_X(B)=P\bigl(X^{-1}(B)\bigr)=P(X\in B).}

Thus a numerical question about XX is first converted to a set of outcomes, and that event is assigned a probability. This is the set-based construction used in the Bertrand calculation below. It follows the distinction in Jagannathan, Chapter 11, Definitions 68–70.

Worked example: the head count

For two independent fair tosses, each outcome in Ω={HH,HT,TH,TT}\Omega=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\} has probability 1/41/4, and ℱ=2Ω\mathcal F=2^\Omega. Define X(HH)=2X(HH)=2, X(HT)=X(TH)=1X(HT)=X(TH)=1, and X(TT)=0X(TT)=0. Then

PX({1})=P({HT,TH})=1/2,P_X(\{1\})=P(\{HT,TH\})=1/2,

PX([1,2])=P({HH,HT,TH})=3/4.P_X([1,2])=P(\{HH,HT,TH\})=3/4.

The distribution assigns probabilities 1/4,1/2,1/41/4,1/2,1/4 to 0,1,20,1,2. It is obtained through inverse images, not by assuming that the three numerical values are equally likely.

Worked example: an indicator

For an event A∈ℱA\in\mathcal F, its indicator is the random variable

𝟏A(ω)={1,ω∈A,0,ω∉A.\mathbf1_A(\omega)=\begin{cases}1,&\omega\in A,\\0,&\omega\notin A.\end{cases}

Its inverse images satisfy 𝟏A−1({1})=A\mathbf1_A^{-1}(\{1\})=A and 𝟏A−1({0})=Ac\mathbf1_A^{-1}(\{0\})=A^c. Consequently, P(𝟏A=1)=P(A)P(\mathbf1_A=1)=P(A).

In the parity model of Section 4 of the main notes, the indicator of an even face is measurable. The numerical face-value function is not measurable relative to that σ-algebra, since the inverse image of {6}\{6\} is the unavailable subset {6}\{6\}.

3. Bertrand’s paradox: sampling maps and induced distributions

4. The induced law is a probability measure

Let X:(Ω,ℱ,P)→(ℝ,ℬ(ℝ))X:(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal B(\mathbb R)) be measurable and define PX(B)=P(X−1(B))P_X(B)=P(X^{-1}(B)). Nonnegativity follows from that of PP, and

If B1,B2,…B_1,B_2,\ldots are pairwise disjoint Borel sets, their inverse images are pairwise disjoint events, and inverse images preserve their union. Therefore

This proves that PXP_X is a probability measure. The same proof applies to the sampling maps Ψj\Psi_j and the induced measures PjP_j on the chord space. Injectivity is unnecessary for this construction: inverse images exist for all functions.

5. A subset of the interval to which no probability can be assigned

Section 4 of the main notes states that the event collection for the uniform model on an interval cannot be the set of all subsets. The construction is given here.

The setting

Let Ω=[0,1)\Omega=[0,1) and define addition modulo 11,

x⊕y={x+y,x+y<1,x+y−1,x+y≥1,x\oplus y=\begin{cases}x+y,&x+y<1,\\x+y-1,&x+y\ge1,\end{cases}

with ⊖\ominus the corresponding subtraction. Under ⊕\oplus the set [0,1)[0,1) is an abelian group; identifying 00 with 11 makes it a circle, and x↦x⊕tx\mapsto x\oplus t is a rotation. For A⊆[0,1)A\subseteq[0,1) write A⊕t={a⊕t:a∈A}A\oplus t=\{a\oplus t:a\in A\}.

Suppose PP is defined on every subset of [0,1)[0,1) and satisfies

  1. P([0,1))=1P([0,1))=1;
  2. countable additivity;
  3. rotation invariance: P(A⊕t)=P(A)P(A\oplus t)=P(A) for all AA and all t∈[0,1)t\in[0,1).

The construction

Let ℚ0=ℚ∩[0,1)\mathbb Q_0=\mathbb Q\cap[0,1), a countable subgroup of ([0,1),⊕)([0,1),\oplus). Define x∼yx\sim y when x⊖y∈ℚ0x\ominus y\in\mathbb Q_0. This is an equivalence relation, and its classes are the cosets x⊕ℚ0x\oplus\mathbb Q_0.

By the axiom of choice, let V⊆[0,1)V\subseteq[0,1) contain exactly one element of each class.

Claim 1: the sets V⊕qV\oplus q, for q∈ℚ0q\in\mathbb Q_0, are pairwise disjoint. Suppose v1⊕q1=v2⊕q2v_1\oplus q_1=v_2\oplus q_2 with v1,v2∈Vv_1,v_2\in V. Then v1⊖v2=q2⊖q1∈ℚ0v_1\ominus v_2=q_2\ominus q_1\in\mathbb Q_0, so v1∼v2v_1\sim v_2. Since VV has one element per class, v1=v2v_1=v_2, and therefore q1=q2q_1=q_2.

Claim 2: these sets cover [0,1)[0,1). Given x∈[0,1)x\in[0,1), let v∈Vv\in V be the representative of the class of xx. Then q=x⊖v∈ℚ0q=x\ominus v\in\mathbb Q_0 and x=v⊕q∈V⊕qx=v\oplus q\in V\oplus q.

So [0,1)[0,1) is the union of countably many pairwise disjoint sets, each a rotation of VV.

The contradiction

Write c=P(V)c=P(V). Rotation invariance gives P(V⊕q)=cP(V\oplus q)=c for every qq, and countable additivity applied to the countable disjoint family of Claim 1, whose union is [0,1)[0,1) by Claim 2, gives

1=P([0,1))=∑q∈ℚ0P(V⊕q)=∑q∈ℚ0c.1=P([0,1))=\sum_{q\in\mathbb Q_0}P(V\oplus q)=\sum_{q\in\mathbb Q_0}c .

If c=0c=0 the right-hand side is 00; if c>0c>0 it is unbounded. Neither equals 11, so no such PP exists, and VV is not Lebesgue measurable. This is Vitali’s theorem (1905).

Which assumption fails

The argument uses all three conditions, and it is instructive to see which one cannot be weakened.

Countable additivity is the operative assumption in dimension one. Banach (1923) showed that a finitely additive rotation-invariant extension of Lebesgue measure to all subsets of the circle does exist. Restricting to finite additivity therefore removes the obstruction here, at the cost of the limit theorems described in Section 5.5 of the main notes; this is the price of de Finetti’s position, noted in Section 5.3 there.

Invariance cannot be dropped without changing the model, since it is what makes the measure uniform. Non-uniform measures on all subsets are unproblematic: a measure concentrated on countably many points is defined on 2[0,1)2^{[0,1)} and is countably additive.

Dimension matters. In ℝ3\mathbb R^3 even finite additivity is insufficient: the Banach–Tarski theorem (1924) shows that no finitely additive isometry-invariant measure defined on all subsets can assign the unit ball a positive finite value.

Why this does not affect the models used in the course

The selection of VV uses the axiom of choice and gives no rule for deciding membership. Solovay (1970) showed it is consistent with Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory together with dependent choice that every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, so no explicit description of such a set is possible. Every event arising in this lecture — intervals, disks, angular sectors, the preimages Ψj−1(E)\Psi_j^{-1}(E) of Section 3, and countable combinations of these — is a Borel set, and the uniform model is defined on the Borel σ-algebra without difficulty.
