01.6 · Transformations of random variables

Data Analytics for AI · Executive M.Tech · IIT Jammu
Previous: expectation · Rigorous supplement · Next: multivariate normal

Questions and objectives

A service manager records two phase durations but needs the distribution of their sum. An engineer records a signed error but reports its square. In the friends’ meeting problem, the decision depends on the absolute gap rather than the two separate arrival times.

In each case, the experiment and its probability measure remain fixed while a new function records a different value. We will derive transformed laws by constructing inverse-image events and integrating, by constructing a joint density with an auxiliary variable, and by applying Jacobians. All application models are stipulated for teaching.

1. Start from the event: the CDF method

Worked example: square a signed measurement error

Let XX be a hypothetical sensor error, uniform on [−2,2][-2,2] in a specified measurement unit. The reporting system uses squared error Y=X2Y=X^2. What is P(Y≤1)P(Y\le1)?

The condition X2≤1X^2\le1 selects −1≤X≤1-1\le X\le1, an interval of length 2 within an interval of length 4. Thus P(Y≤1)=1/2P(Y\le1)=1/2. For a general threshold 0≤y≤40\le y\le4,

{Y≤y}={−y≤X≤y},FY(y)=∫−yy14dx=y2.\{Y\le y\}=\{-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y\},\qquad F_Y(y)=\int_{-\sqrt y}^{\sqrt y}\frac14\,dx=\frac{\sqrt y}{2}.

Including thresholds outside the range,

FY(y)={0,y<0,y/2,0≤y≤4,1,y>4.F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}0,&y<0,\\\sqrt y/2,&0\le y\le4,\\1,&y>4.\end{cases}

Differentiation gives

fY(y)=14y,0<y<4,f_Y(y)=\frac1{4\sqrt y},\qquad0<y<4,

and zero elsewhere. The density is unbounded near zero but integrable: ∫04(4y)−1dy=1\int_0^4(4\sqrt y)^{-1}dy=1. There is no atom at zero since P(X=0)=0P(X=0)=0.

General construction

For measurable gg, define Y=g(X)Y=g(X). Its induced law is

PY(B)=P(g(X)∈B),FY(y)=P(g(X)≤y).P_Y(B)=P(g(X)\in B),\qquad F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\le y).

If XX has a density,

FY(y)=∫{x:g(x)≤y}fX(x)dx.F_Y(y)=\int_{\{x:g(x)\le y\}}f_X(x)\,dx.

For Z=g(X,Y)Z=g(X,Y) and an available joint density,

FZ(z)=∬{(x,y):g(x,y)≤z}fX,Y(x,y)dxdy.F_Z(z)=\iint_{\{(x,y):g(x,y)\le z\}}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy.

First identify the transformed range, then the inverse-image region and its bounds, then integrate. Differentiate the CDF where appropriate only after checking whether the transformed law has atoms. A smooth PDF is not guaranteed merely because the original variable has one.

2. A one-dimensional Jacobian

Worked example: convert and shift a duration

Let XX be uniform on [0,2][0,2] hours and Y=10+60XY=10+60X minutes. Then 10<Y<13010<Y<130 and x=(y−10)/60x=(y-10)/60. Since a one-minute interval of YY corresponds to 1/601/60 hour of XX,

fY(y)=fX(y−1060)|dxdy|=12160=1120.f_Y(y)=f_X\left(\frac{y-10}{60}\right)\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right| =\frac12\frac1{60}=\frac1{120}.

It integrates to 1 over an interval of length 120. Changing the numerical scale changes density height, preserving probabilities.

If gg is a differentiable, strictly monotone map with a differentiable inverse hh and nonzero derivative on the relevant support interior, the change-of-variable formula is

fY(y)=fX(h(y))|h′(y)|,f_Y(y)=f_X(h(y))\,|h'(y)|,

on the transformed support and zero elsewhere. The multiplier is the absolute derivative of the inverse transformation. Endpoint exceptions of probability zero can be treated separately.

For a transformation with finitely many smooth inverse branches, add their contributions:

fY(y)=∑x:g(x)=yfX(x)|g′(x)|,f_Y(y)=\sum_{x:g(x)=y}\frac{f_X(x)}{|g'(x)|},

at regular values with nonzero derivatives. For Y=X2Y=X^2 and 0<y<40<y<4, there are two roots ±y\pm\sqrt y:

fY(y)=1/42y+1/42y=14y.f_Y(y)=\frac{1/4}{2\sqrt y}+\frac{1/4}{2\sqrt y}=\frac1{4\sqrt y}.

Using only the positive root would lose half the probability. At the critical value zero, use the CDF to check for an atom rather than substituting into the branch formula.

3. Sum of two durations: integrate over an event

Worked example: two independent processing phases

Stipulate independent X,Y∼U(0,1)X,Y\sim U(0,1) hours. Let S=X+YS=X+Y be total processing time, with phases sequential and no additional waiting. The joint density is 1 on the unit square. For 0<s<10<s<1, the region x+y≤sx+y\le s is a right triangle of area s2/2s^2/2. For 1≤s<21\le s<2, its complement in the square is a triangle of area (2−s)2/2(2-s)^2/2. Hence

FS(s)={0,s≤0,s2/2,0<s<1,1−(2−s)2/2,1≤s<2,1,s≥2,F_S(s)=\begin{cases} 0,&s\le0,\\s^2/2,&0<s<1,\\1-(2-s)^2/2,&1\le s<2,\\1,&s\ge2, \end{cases}

and

fS(s)={s,0<s<1,2−s,1≤s<2,0,otherwise.f_S(s)=\begin{cases}s,&0<s<1,\\2-s,&1\le s<2,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The PDF integrates to 1/2+1/2=11/2+1/2=1. It is not uniform even though both inputs are uniform. There are more original pairs near a total of one hour than near a total of zero or two hours.

4. Construct a joint density, then marginalize

Rework the sum using an auxiliary variable

A map from two values to their sum loses information. To use a square Jacobian matrix, retain an auxiliary variable: set

S=X+Y,V=X.S=X+Y,\qquad V=X.

The inverse is x=vx=v, y=s−vy=s-v. Its Jacobian matrix and absolute determinant are

∂(x,y)∂(s,v)=(011−1),|det⁡|=1.\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(s,v)}= \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad |\det|=1.

The original inequalities 0<x<10<x<1, 0<y<10<y<1 become

0<v<1,0<s−v<1⇔max⁡(0,s−1)<v<min⁡(1,s).0<v<1,\quad0<s-v<1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \max(0,s-1)<v<\min(1,s).

Thus fS,V(s,v)=1f_{S,V}(s,v)=1 on that region and zero elsewhere. Integrate out vv:

fS(s)=∫max⁡(0,s−1)min⁡(1,s)1dv,f_S(s)=\int_{\max(0,s-1)}^{\min(1,s)}1\,dv,

when the upper bound exceeds the lower. The width is ss for 0<s<10<s<1 and 2−s2-s for 1<s<21<s<2, reproducing the CDF result.

For a general joint density, the same construction gives

fX+Y(s)=∫−∞∞fX,Y(v,s−v)dv.f_{X+Y}(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X,Y}(v,s-v)\,dv.

Under independence, this becomes the convolution formula

fX+Y(s)=∫−∞∞fX(v)fY(s−v)dv.f_{X+Y}(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(v)f_Y(s-v)\,dv.

Dependence does not prevent the first formula; it prevents replacing the joint density by the product without justification.

Formal two-dimensional change of variables

Suppose (X,Y)(X,Y) has a joint density and the transformation (U,V)=g(X,Y)(U,V)=g(X,Y) is a one-to-one continuously differentiable map between open support regions, with continuously differentiable inverse (x,y)=h(u,v)(x,y)=h(u,v) and nonzero Jacobian determinant. Then

fU,V(u,v)=fX,Y(h(u,v))|det∂(x,y)∂(u,v)|.f_{U,V}(u,v)=f_{X,Y}(h(u,v)) \left|\det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|.

A small region in the new coordinates corresponds to an old-coordinate area multiplied by the inverse determinant. Probability is preserved, so density acquires that factor. State the transformed support as part of the result. Null boundaries can be omitted; branch maps require adding branch contributions. A dimension-reducing map such as (x,y)↦x+y(x,y)\mapsto x+y has no square determinant by itself.

5. A nonconstant Jacobian: total duration and its share

Worked example: time spent in the first of two phases

Let X,YX,Y be independent with density e−xe^{-x} on x>0x>0, zero otherwise, using a time unit in which the rate is 1. This exponential model is a stipulation. Define

S=X+Y,R=XX+Y.S=X+Y,\qquad R=\frac{X}{X+Y}.

The total is positive and the fraction lies between 0 and 1. The inverse map is x=rsx=rs, y=(1−r)sy=(1-r)s. Compute rather than guess the determinant:

∂(x,y)∂(s,r)=(rs1−r−s),det⁡=−rs−s(1−r)=−s.\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(s,r)}= \begin{pmatrix}r&s\\1-r&-s\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det=-rs-s(1-r)=-s.

Thus

fS,R(s,r)=e−rse−(1−r)ss=se−s,s>0,0<r<1.f_{S,R}(s,r)=e^{-rs}e^{-(1-r)s}s=se^{-s},\quad s>0,\ 0<r<1.

Marginalization gives

fS(s)=∫01se−sdr=se−s,fR(r)=∫0∞se−sds=1.f_S(s)=\int_0^1se^{-s}\,dr=se^{-s},\qquad f_R(r)=\int_0^\infty se^{-s}\,ds=1.

Integration by parts yields ∫0∞se−sds=1\int_0^\infty se^{-s}ds=1. The joint density factors into its marginals on the product support, so the total and share are independent in this particular model. This conclusion depends on the stipulated equal-rate exponential inputs; it does not follow from the definitions of total and share alone.

For example,

P(S≤2,R≤1/4)=∫02∫01/4se−sdrds=14[1−3e−2].P(S\le2,R\le1/4)=\int_0^2\int_0^{1/4}se^{-s}\,dr\,ds =\frac14[1-3e^{-2}].

6. Return to the meeting-time gap

For independent uniform arrivals TA,TBT_A,T_B in [0,1][0,1], take the signed difference Z=TB−TAZ=T_B-T_A and retain V=TAV=T_A. Then tA=vt_A=v, tB=v+zt_B=v+z and the inverse determinant has absolute value 1. The joint support is

max⁡(0,−z)<v<min⁡(1,1−z).\max(0,-z)<v<\min(1,1-z).

Integrating its width gives fZ(z)=1−|z|f_Z(z)=1-|z| for −1<z<1-1<z<1. The absolute gap D=|Z|D=|Z| has two preimages for d>0d>0, so

fD(d)=fZ(d)+fZ(−d)=2(1−d),0<d<1.f_D(d)=f_Z(d)+f_Z(-d)=2(1-d),\quad0<d<1.

This agrees with the band-area CDF route in 01.4. Both routes construct the same induced law under the same original measure.

7. Generate variables by transforming simple ones

Worked example: generate a nonuniform arrival time

Let U∼U(0,1)U\sim U(0,1) and define T=1−1−UT=1-\sqrt{1-U}. For 0≤t≤10\le t\le1,

P(T≤t)=P(1−1−U≤t)=P(U≤1−(1−t)2)=2t−t2.\begin{aligned} P(T\le t)&=P(1-\sqrt{1-U}\le t)\\ &=P(U\le1-(1-t)^2)=2t-t^2. \end{aligned}

Differentiation gives fT(t)=2(1−t)f_T(t)=2(1-t), the early-arrival model. Two independent uniform inputs transformed separately produce independent arrivals with these marginals.

More generally, for a CDF FF define its generalized inverse F−1(u)=inf⁡{x:F(x)≥u}F^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x:F(x)\ge u\} for 0<u<10<u<1. Then F−1(U)F^{-1}(U) has CDF FF. The generalized inverse also handles discrete laws: if P(N=0,1,2)=(0.5,0.3,0.2)P(N=0,1,2)=(0.5,0.3,0.2), assign 0 for U<0.5U<0.5, 1 for 0.5≤U<0.80.5\le U<0.8, and 2 otherwise.

Generating both variables from the same uniform input can create dependence even when both marginals are correct. The multivariate-normal construction in 01.7 will deliberately combine independent inputs to obtain a chosen covariance.

8. A continuous input can create a mixed output

Return to DD and define the capped wait W=min⁡(D,1/4)W=\min(D,1/4). For 0≤w<1/40\le w<1/4, FW(w)=2w−w2F_W(w)=2w-w^2. At and above 1/41/4, FW(w)=1F_W(w)=1. The CDF jumps by

1−FW((1/4)−)=1−716=916.1-F_W((1/4)^-)=1-\frac7{16}=\frac9{16}.

Thus WW has a continuous component on (0,1/4)(0,1/4) and a point mass 9/169/16 at 1/41/4. Differentiating away from the jump cannot recover that atom. This is why the CDF and inverse-image construction is the general starting point.
