The central limit theorem · A complete characteristic-function proof

1. The question: why should an average look normal?

Suppose a calibration procedure produces independent signed measurement errors X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\ldots under the same operating conditions. As a hypothetical example, each error could be uniform on (−1,1)(-1,1): its distribution is flat, not bell-shaped. Averaging reduces variability. Does the distribution of the average also acquire a predictable shape?

For uniform errors, E[Xj]=0E[X_j]=0 and Var⁡(Xj)=1/3\operatorname{Var}(X_j)=1/3. Independence gives

E[X‾n]=0,Var⁡(X‾n)=13n,X‾n=1n∑j=1nXj.E[\bar X_n]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac1{3n},\qquad \bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nX_j.

Thus X‾n\bar X_n concentrates near zero. To study the shape of its fluctuations, rather than watch it collapse toward zero, rescale it:

3nX‾n.\sqrt{3n}\,\bar X_n.

This has variance one for every nn. The central limit theorem says its distribution tends to N(0,1)N(0,1). The result is much broader than uniform errors.

This standalone note proves the classical independent, identically distributed, finite-variance central limit theorem. Other central limit theorems allow nonidentical or dependent observations under additional conditions; they are not asserted here. We will prove every transform calculation and then justify the passage from transforms to CDFs. The final analytic step is developed explicitly by Gaussian smoothing, so the proof does not stop at citing a continuity theorem.

Prerequisites. Expectation, variance, independence, integration and elementary differentiation. We state the dominated convergence and integration-interchange facts when used. Complex numbers enter only through eit=cos⁡t+isin⁡te^{it}=\cos t+i\sin t and an explicitly defined logarithm near 1.

2. State exactly what must be proved

Theorem. Let X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\ldots be independent and identically distributed real random variables with

E[X1]=μ,0<σ2=E[(X1−μ)2]<∞.E[X_1]=\mu,\qquad 0<\sigma^2=E[(X_1-\mu)^2]<\infty.

Define

Zj=Xj−μσ,Sn=1n∑j=1nZj=∑j=1nXj−nμσn=n(X‾n−μ)σ.Z_j=\frac{X_j-\mu}{\sigma},\qquad S_n=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{j=1}^nZ_j =\frac{\sum_{j=1}^nX_j-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n} =\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}.

Then, for every real xx,

limn→∞P(Sn≤x)=Φ(x),Φ(x)=∫−∞xe−u2/22πdu.\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}P(S_n\le x)=\Phi(x)},\qquad \Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x\frac{e^{-u^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}du.

This is convergence in distribution, written Sn→dN(0,1)S_n\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1). In general the CDF definition requires convergence only at continuity points of the limiting CDF. Here Φ\Phi is continuous everywhere.

The conditions have distinct roles:

  • Independence lets us factor a joint expectation into a product.
  • Identical distribution makes every factor the same function.
  • Finite variance supplies a controlled second-order expansion near zero.
  • Positive variance permits division by σ\sigma and gives a nondegenerate limit.

A density is not required: the theorem applies to discrete, continuous and mixed laws. A finite third moment and a finite MGF are also not required.

Why the normalization is n\sqrt n

Each ZjZ_j has mean 0 and variance 1. Independence implies zero cross-covariances, so

E[Sn]=0,Var⁡(Sn)=1n∑j=1nVar⁡(Zj)=1.E[S_n]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n)=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\operatorname{Var}(Z_j)=1.

The unscaled sum has variance nn; dividing it by n\sqrt n keeps variance at 1. Dividing the sum by nn instead produces the sample average, whose variance tends to zero. These are different questions.

3. Characteristic functions: definition and the rules we need

For any real random variable YY, define

φY(t)=E[eitY]=E[cos⁡(tY)]+iE[sin⁡(tY)],t∈ℝ.\varphi_Y(t)=E[e^{itY}]=E[\cos(tY)]+iE[\sin(tY)],\qquad t\in\mathbb R.

An expectation of a complex variable means the expectations of its real and imaginary parts. The parameter tt is chosen by us; it is not a random observation.

Existence, value at zero and boundedness

Because |eitY|=1|e^{itY}|=1, the expectation always exists and

φY(0)=1,|φY(t)|≤E[|eitY|]=1.\varphi_Y(0)=1,\qquad |\varphi_Y(t)|\le E[|e^{itY}|]=1.

This is why characteristic functions work even when E[etY]E[e^{tY}] is infinite for every positive tt. No density or moment assumption is needed for existence.

Scaling and shifting

For constants a,ba,b,

φaY+b(t)=E[eit(aY+b)]=eitbE[ei(at)Y]=eitbφY(at).\varphi_{aY+b}(t)=E[e^{it(aY+b)}] =e^{itb}E[e^{i(at)Y}]=e^{itb}\varphi_Y(at).

Multiplying the variable by aa multiplies the transform’s argument by aa.

Independent sums

If Y1,…,YnY_1,\ldots,Y_n are independent, their bounded measurable functions eitYje^{itY_j} are independent, so

φY1+⋯+Yn(t)=E[∏j=1neitYj]=∏j=1nE[eitYj]=∏j=1nφYj(t).\begin{aligned} \varphi_{Y_1+\cdots+Y_n}(t) &=E\left[\prod_{j=1}^n e^{itY_j}\right]\\ &=\prod_{j=1}^nE[e^{itY_j}] =\prod_{j=1}^n\varphi_{Y_j}(t). \end{aligned}

For complex expectations, the factorization follows by expanding into bounded real and imaginary parts and using the ordinary independence rule. Zero covariance alone is not enough for this step.

4. Identify the characteristic function of the target normal law

We must know which function corresponds to N(0,1)N(0,1); recognizing a familiar exponential without justification would leave a gap.

Let G∼N(0,1)G\sim N(0,1) with density g(x)=e−x2/2/2πg(x)=e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}, and write ψ(t)=E[eitG]\psi(t)=E[e^{itG}]. Since E|G|<∞E|G|<\infty, differentiation under the integral gives

ψ′(t)=i∫ℝxeitxg(x)dx.\psi'(t)=i\int_{\mathbb R}xe^{itx}g(x)dx.

To justify it, the difference quotient has absolute value at most |x||x|, using |eihx−1|≤|hx||e^{ihx}-1|\le |hx|. The function |x|g(x)|x|g(x) is integrable, so dominated convergence applies.

Since g′(x)=−xg(x)g'(x)=-xg(x), integration by parts yields

∫ℝxeitxg(x)dx=−∫ℝeitxg′(x)dx=−[eitxg(x)]−∞∞+it∫ℝeitxg(x)dx=itψ(t).\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb R}xe^{itx}g(x)dx &=-\int_{\mathbb R}e^{itx}g'(x)dx\\ &=-[e^{itx}g(x)]_{-\infty}^{\infty} +it\int_{\mathbb R}e^{itx}g(x)dx\\ &=it\psi(t). \end{aligned}

The boundary term vanishes because |eitxg(x)|=g(x)→0|e^{itx}g(x)|=g(x)\to0. Therefore

ψ′(t)=−tψ(t),ψ(0)=1.\psi'(t)=-t\psi(t),\qquad\psi(0)=1.

Multiplying by et2/2e^{t^2/2} shows that ddt(et2/2ψ(t))=0\frac d{dt}(e^{t^2/2}\psi(t))=0. Its value is 1 at zero, giving

φG(t)=e−t2/2.\boxed{\varphi_G(t)=e^{-t^2/2}.}

The scaling rule also gives φτG(t)=e−τ2t2/2\varphi_{\tau G}(t)=e^{-\tau^2t^2/2} for τ>0\tau>0.

5. The essential local expansion: finite variance is enough

Let ZZ have the common law of the standardized ZjZ_j. We know E[Z]=0E[Z]=0 and E[Z2]=1E[Z^2]=1. We want to prove

φZ(u)=1−u22+o(u2)(u→0).\boxed{\varphi_Z(u)=1-\frac{u^2}{2}+o(u^2)\quad(u\to0).}

The notation r(u)=o(u2)r(u)=o(u^2) means r(u)/u2→0r(u)/u^2\to0 as u→0u\to0, u≠0u\ne0. The remainder may be complex; convergence is in absolute value.

A pointwise Taylor identity with a bounded remainder

For every real vv, Taylor’s formula with its integral remainder gives

eiv=1+iv−v2∫01(1−s)eisvds.e^{iv}=1+iv-v^2\int_0^1(1-s)e^{isv}ds.

One way to obtain it is to integrate the second derivative of s↦eisvs\mapsto e^{isv} twice from 0 to 1. Since ∫01(1−s)ds=1/2\int_0^1(1-s)ds=1/2, rearrange it as

eiv=1+iv−v22+v2r(v),e^{iv}=1+iv-\frac{v^2}{2}+v^2r(v),

where

r(v)=∫01(1−s)(1−eisv)ds.r(v)=\int_0^1(1-s)(1-e^{isv})ds.

Two properties are crucial:

|r(v)|≤∫01(1−s)2ds=1,|r(v)|\le\int_0^1(1-s)\,2\,ds=1,

and r(v)→0r(v)\to0 as v→0v\to0, by bounded convergence in the integral. The bound holds for all real vv, not only small ones.

Substitute the random variable and take expectations

Set v=uZv=uZ in the identity:

eiuZ=1+iuZ−u2Z22+u2Z2r(uZ).e^{iuZ}=1+iuZ-\frac{u^2Z^2}{2}+u^2Z^2r(uZ).

All terms are integrable: finite second moment implies E|Z|≤E[Z2]=1E|Z|\le\sqrt{E[Z^2]}=1, and |Z2r(uZ)|≤Z2|Z^2r(uZ)|\le Z^2. Therefore

φZ(u)=1+iuE[Z]−u22E[Z2]+u2E[Z2r(uZ)]=1−u22+u2ε(u),\varphi_Z(u)=1+iuE[Z]-\frac{u^2}{2}E[Z^2] +u^2E[Z^2r(uZ)] =1-\frac{u^2}{2}+u^2\varepsilon(u),

where ε(u)=E[Z2r(uZ)]\varepsilon(u)=E[Z^2r(uZ)].

For each finite realized value of ZZ, r(uZ)→0r(uZ)\to0. The integrable bound Z2Z^2 does not depend on uu. The dominated convergence theorem—pointwise convergence plus an integrable absolute bound permits passing the limit inside an expectation—now gives

ε(u)→0.\varepsilon(u)\longrightarrow0.

This proves the required expansion without assuming E|Z|3<∞E|Z|^3<\infty. A third-order remainder bounded by a constant times |uZ|3|uZ|^3 would demand an extra moment that the theorem does not assume. The bounded second-order remainder avoids that problem.

Stopping point. Check that the zero mean removes the linear term, the unit variance fixes the quadratic term, and the remainder estimate uses exactly the finite second moment.

6. The transform of the standardized sum

Combining the scaling and independent-sum rules,

φSn(t)=E[exp(itn∑j=1nZj)]=∏j=1nφZj(t/n)=[φZ(t/n)]n.\begin{aligned} \varphi_{S_n}(t) &=E\left[\exp\left(\frac{it}{\sqrt n}\sum_{j=1}^nZ_j\right)\right]\\ &=\prod_{j=1}^n\varphi_{Z_j}(t/\sqrt n)\\ &=\left[\varphi_Z(t/\sqrt n)\right]^n. \end{aligned}

Fix a real tt. For t≠0t\ne0, t/n→0t/\sqrt n\to0, so the expansion gives

φZ(t/n)=1−t22n+t2nε(t/n)=1+an,\varphi_Z(t/\sqrt n) =1-\frac{t^2}{2n}+\frac{t^2}{n}\varepsilon(t/\sqrt n) =1+a_n,

with

nan=−t2/2+t2ε(t/n)→−t2/2.na_n=-t^2/2+t^2\varepsilon(t/\sqrt n)\longrightarrow-t^2/2.

In particular, an=O(1/n)a_n=O(1/n): for this fixed tt, its magnitude is eventually bounded by a constant divided by nn. We must evaluate (1+an)n(1+a_n)^n, remembering that ana_n can be complex.

7. Justify the exponential limit for complex factors

For |z|<1|z|<1, define the logarithm near 1 by its convergent power series:

log⁡(1+z)=z−z22+z33−⋯.\log(1+z)=z-\frac{z^2}{2}+\frac{z^3}{3}-\cdots.

For |z|≤1/2|z|\le1/2,

|log⁡(1+z)−z|≤∑j=2∞|z|j=|z|21−|z|≤2|z|2.|\log(1+z)-z| \le\sum_{j=2}^\infty |z|^j =\frac{|z|^2}{1-|z|}\le2|z|^2.

This local logarithm satisfies exp⁡(log⁡(1+z))=1+z\exp(\log(1+z))=1+z. There is no global choice of a complex logarithm involved: for sufficiently large nn, every 1+an1+a_n lies in this single neighborhood of 1.

Since an=O(1/n)a_n=O(1/n),

|nlog⁡(1+an)−nan|≤2n|an|2→0.|n\log(1+a_n)-na_n|\le2n|a_n|^2\longrightarrow0.

Consequently,

nlog⁡(1+an)→−t2/2,n\log(1+a_n)\longrightarrow-t^2/2,

and continuity of the exponential gives

φSn(t)=(1+an)n=exp⁡{nlog⁡(1+an)}→e−t2/2.\varphi_{S_n}(t)=(1+a_n)^n =\exp\{n\log(1+a_n)\}\longrightarrow e^{-t^2/2}.

For t=0t=0, both sides are 1 for every nn. We have proved the pointwise transform limit for every real tt:

φSn(t)→φG(t).\boxed{\varphi_{S_n}(t)\longrightarrow\varphi_G(t).}

Here tt is fixed while nn grows. We have not asserted uniform convergence over all tt, convergence of densities, or a finite-sample error bound.

8. Why this proves convergence of distributions

The usual final step invokes Lévy’s continuity theorem: if characteristic functions converge pointwise to a function continuous at zero, the limit is a characteristic function and the corresponding laws converge in distribution to its law. Our limit e−t2/2e^{-t^2/2} is continuous at zero and is the characteristic function of G∼N(0,1)G\sim N(0,1). Therefore Sn→dGS_n\xrightarrow{d}G.

That completes the standard proof. To explain the analytic bridge rather than leave it as a named result, the next section proves the exact implication needed here directly. It uses the already established bound E[Sn2]=1E[S_n^2]=1 and Gaussian smoothing. It does not claim to prove every version of the continuity theorem.

9. Prove the final bridge by Gaussian smoothing

9.1 Add a small independent normal perturbation

Fix δ>0\delta>0. On an enlarged probability space, take G∼N(0,1)G\sim N(0,1) independent of all the SnS_n, and set

Yn,δ=Sn+δG.Y_{n,\delta}=S_n+\delta G.

We introduce this perturbation only as a proof device. Even if SnS_n is discrete, Yn,δY_{n,\delta} has a smooth density. Write the N(0,δ2)N(0,\delta^2) density as

qδ(z)=12πδexp⁡(−z22δ2).q_\delta(z)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\delta}\exp\left(-\frac{z^2}{2\delta^2}\right).

Conditioning on SnS_n gives

fn,δ(x)=E[qδ(x−Sn)].f_{n,\delta}(x)=E[q_\delta(x-S_n)].

To verify this rather than assume it, integrate over any Borel set BB:

P(Sn+δG∈B)=E[∫Bqδ(x−Sn)dx]=∫BE[qδ(x−Sn)]dx.P(S_n+\delta G\in B)=E\left[\int_B q_\delta(x-S_n)dx\right] =\int_B E[q_\delta(x-S_n)]dx.

The interchange is valid for nonnegative integrands (Tonelli’s theorem).

9.2 A Gaussian Fourier identity, derived explicitly

The normal characteristic-function calculation from section 4, applied to a normal variable with variance 1/δ21/\delta^2, gives

∫ℝe−itze−δ2t2/2dt=2πδe−z2/(2δ2).\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-itz}e^{-\delta^2t^2/2}dt =\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\delta}e^{-z^2/(2\delta^2)}.

Indeed multiplying the integrand by δ/2π\delta/\sqrt{2\pi} makes it the density-weighted characteristic-function integral for N(0,1/δ2)N(0,1/\delta^2) at argument −z-z. Thus

qδ(z)=12π∫ℝe−itze−δ2t2/2dt.q_\delta(z)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-itz}e^{-\delta^2t^2/2}dt.

This is a directly verified Gaussian identity, not an assumed general Fourier inversion theorem.

9.3 Pass the transform limit through an integrable kernel

Substituting z=x−Snz=x-S_n yields

fn,δ(x)=12πE[∫e−it(x−Sn)e−δ2t2/2dt]=12π∫e−itxφSn(t)e−δ2t2/2dt.\begin{aligned} f_{n,\delta}(x) &=\frac1{2\pi}E\left[\int e^{-it(x-S_n)}e^{-\delta^2t^2/2}dt\right]\\ &=\frac1{2\pi}\int e^{-itx}\varphi_{S_n}(t)e^{-\delta^2t^2/2}dt. \end{aligned}

The interchange is valid because the absolute integrand is bounded by e−δ2t2/2e^{-\delta^2t^2/2}, whose integral is finite. This is the absolute-integrability form of Fubini’s theorem.

Now |φSn(t)|≤1|\varphi_{S_n}(t)|\le1 and φSn(t)→e−t2/2\varphi_{S_n}(t)\to e^{-t^2/2}. Dominated convergence gives, for each xx,

fn,δ(x)→12π∫e−itxe−(1+δ2)t2/2dt=q1+δ2(x).f_{n,\delta}(x)\longrightarrow \frac1{2\pi}\int e^{-itx}e^{-(1+\delta^2)t^2/2}dt =q_{\sqrt{1+\delta^2}}(x).

So the smoothed densities converge pointwise to the density of N(0,1+δ2)N(0,1+\delta^2).

9.4 From these densities to their CDFs: control the tails

Pointwise convergence of densities must not simply be integrated over an infinite interval without justification. Here we have two useful bounds.

First, fn,δ(x)≤1/(2πδ)f_{n,\delta}(x)\le1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\delta), uniformly in n,xn,x, since it is an average of qδq_\delta. On any bounded interval, this constant is integrable. Second, independence and centering give

E[Yn,δ]=0,E[Yn,δ2]=1+δ2.E[Y_{n,\delta}]=0,\qquad E[Y_{n,\delta}^2]=1+\delta^2.

For any centered variable VV, V2≥R2𝟏{|V|≥R}V^2\ge R^2\mathbf1_{\{|V|\ge R\}} implies the Chebyshev bound

P(|V|≥R)≤E[V2]/R2.P(|V|\ge R)\le E[V^2]/R^2.

Fix xx and choose R>|x|R>|x|. Let FδF_\delta be the CDF of N(0,1+δ2)N(0,1+\delta^2). Split each CDF integral at −R-R. Then

|P(Yn,δ≤x)−Fδ(x)|≤2(1+δ2)R2+|∫−Rx[fn,δ(u)−q1+δ2(u)]du|.\left|P(Y_{n,\delta}\le x)-F_\delta(x)\right| \le\frac{2(1+\delta^2)}{R^2} +\left|\int_{-R}^x[f_{n,\delta}(u)-q_{\sqrt{1+\delta^2}}(u)]du\right|.

The bounded-interval integral tends to zero by dominated convergence. Take n→∞n\to\infty first and R→∞R\to\infty afterward. We obtain

P(Yn,δ≤x)→Fδ(x)=Φ(x1+δ2).P(Y_{n,\delta}\le x)\longrightarrow F_\delta(x)=\Phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+\delta^2}}\right).

9.5 Remove the perturbation using event inclusions

For any η>0\eta>0, if Sn≤xS_n\le x and |δG|≤η|\delta G|\le\eta, then Yn,δ≤x+ηY_{n,\delta}\le x+\eta. Conversely, if Yn,δ≤x−ηY_{n,\delta}\le x-\eta and |δG|≤η|\delta G|\le\eta, then Sn≤xS_n\le x. These inclusions imply

P(Yn,δ≤x−η)−P(|δG|>η)≤P(Sn≤x)≤P(Yn,δ≤x+η)+P(|δG|>η).P(Y_{n,\delta}\le x-\eta)-P(|\delta G|>\eta) \le P(S_n\le x) \le P(Y_{n,\delta}\le x+\eta)+P(|\delta G|>\eta).

Chebyshev gives P(|δG|>η)≤δ2/η2P(|\delta G|>\eta)\le\delta^2/\eta^2. Keep δ,η\delta,\eta fixed while taking lower and upper limits in nn:

liminfnP(Sn≤x)≥Φ(x−η1+δ2)−δ2η2,limsupnP(Sn≤x)≤Φ(x+η1+δ2)+δ2η2.\begin{aligned} \liminf_n P(S_n\le x)&\ge \Phi\left(\frac{x-\eta}{\sqrt{1+\delta^2}}\right)-\frac{\delta^2}{\eta^2},\\ \limsup_n P(S_n\le x)&\le \Phi\left(\frac{x+\eta}{\sqrt{1+\delta^2}}\right)+\frac{\delta^2}{\eta^2}. \end{aligned}

Finally choose η=δ\eta=\sqrt\delta and let δ↓0\delta\downarrow0. Then η→0\eta\to0, δ2/η2=δ→0\delta^2/\eta^2=\delta\to0, and continuity of Φ\Phi makes both outer bounds tend to Φ(x)\Phi(x). Therefore

P(Sn≤x)→Φ(x)for every real x.\boxed{P(S_n\le x)\longrightarrow\Phi(x)\quad\text{for every real }x.}

This establishes the final distributional conclusion without assuming that the original sums have densities. The order of limits matters: first n→∞n\to\infty with smoothing fixed, then remove the smoothing.

10. A worked example: uniform errors

Return to Xj∼U(−1,1)X_j\sim U(-1,1). Their mean is zero, variance is 1/31/3, and direct integration gives

φX(t)=12∫−11eitxdx=sin⁡tt(t≠0),φX(0)=1.\varphi_X(t)=\frac12\int_{-1}^1 e^{itx}dx =\frac{\sin t}{t}\quad(t\ne0),\qquad\varphi_X(0)=1.

The standardized summand is Zj=3XjZ_j=\sqrt3X_j, so

φZ(t)=sin⁡(3t)3t.\varphi_Z(t)=\frac{\sin(\sqrt3t)}{\sqrt3t}.

Consequently the characteristic function of Sn=3nX‾nS_n=\sqrt{3n}\bar X_n is

φSn(t)=[sin⁡(3/nt)3/nt]n,\varphi_{S_n}(t)=\left[\frac{\sin(\sqrt{3/n}\,t)}{\sqrt{3/n}\,t}\right]^n,

with the ratio defined as 1 at zero. The expansion sin⁡v/v=1−v2/6+o(v2)\sin v/v=1-v^2/6+o(v^2) makes the bracket 1−t2/(2n)+o(1/n)1-t^2/(2n)+o(1/n), reproducing the general proof.

For n=100n=100, a normal approximation to a tolerance probability is

P(|X‾100|≤0.1)=P(|S100|≤3)≈2Φ(3)−1≈0.917.P(|\bar X_{100}|\le0.1) =P(|S_{100}|\le\sqrt3) \approx2\Phi(\sqrt3)-1\approx0.917.

This is an approximation, not an exact probability or an error guarantee. The original observations are still uniform. Use the interactive demonstration to compare individual observations, sample means and standardized means.

11. What the proof establishes, and what it does not

The law of large numbers and the CLT answer different questions. Chebyshev already gives P(|X‾n−μ|>ϵ)≤σ2/(nϵ2)→0P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\epsilon)\le\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)\to0: the average approaches its mean in probability. The CLT describes the distribution of the remaining fluctuations after multiplication by n/σ\sqrt n/\sigma.

There is no universal “30 observations” guarantee. The proof is asymptotic and gives no approximation error for a specific nn. Skewness and rare large observations can affect finite-sample accuracy. A quantitative bound requires additional work and conditions.

Density convergence is not claimed. For Bernoulli inputs, every finite sum is discrete and has no ordinary density, yet its standardized CDF can converge to the normal CDF. Histogram shapes are illustrations, not the theorem’s statement.

Independence cannot be replaced by a visual impression. If all XjX_j are the same nonconstant variable XX, then the average equals XX for every nn; its variance does not shrink. The independent-product step would be false.

Zero variance is a separate trivial case. If σ2=0\sigma^2=0, then Xj=μX_j=\mu almost surely. The average is constant, and the displayed standardized formula is undefined.

An estimated standard deviation is an additional step. This proof uses the population σ\sigma. Replacing it with a consistent sample standard deviation requires a further convergence argument such as Slutsky’s theorem; it is not part of the derivation above.

The characteristic function avoids an unnecessary MGF assumption. If H=eGH=e^G with G∼N(0,1)G\sim N(0,1), then E[H]=e1/2E[H]=e^{1/2} and E[H2]=e2E[H^2]=e^2 are finite, but E[etH]=∞E[e^{tH}]=\infty for every t>0t>0. Indeed its defining integrand is proportional to exp⁡(teg−g2/2)\exp(te^g-g^2/2), which grows without bound as g→∞g\to\infty. Independent copies still meet the finite-variance CLT assumptions after centering and scaling.
