Module 01 · Fundamentals of Probability and Random Signal Theory

Main lecture notes · Probability-space supplement

This supplement gives proofs and qualifications for the calculations in Lecture 01.2. The main notes contain the definitions needed for routine problem solving. All application models remain stipulated teaching models. Independent exercises appear without solutions.

1. Laws, PMFs and densities

Let (Ω,ℱ,P)(\Omega,\mathcal F,P) be a probability space and X:Ω→ℝX:\Omega\to\mathbb R a measurable function. Its law is

PX(B)=P(X∈B),B∈ℬ(ℝ).P_X(B)=P(X\in B),\qquad B\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R).

If a countable set SS satisfies P(X∈S)=1P(X\in S)=1, the law is discrete. Countable additivity applied to disjoint events {X=x}\{X=x\} gives

PX(B)=∑x∈S∩BpX(x),pX(x)=P(X=x).P_X(B)=\sum_{x\in S\cap B}p_X(x),\qquad p_X(x)=P(X=x).

In particular, ∑x∈SpX(x)=1\sum_{x\in S}p_X(x)=1. The PMF can be set to zero off SS. XX may take values outside SS on a PP-null set of outcomes; the countable support describes where the law places mass.

A law is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure if it admits a measurable nonnegative density fXf_X satisfying

PX(B)=∫BfX(x)dxfor every Borel B.P_X(B)=\int_B f_X(x)\,dx\quad\text{for every Borel }B.

It follows that ∫ℝfX=1\int_{\mathbb R}f_X=1. Densities differing only on a set of Lebesgue measure zero describe the same law. Their values at individual endpoints have no effect on probabilities.

Why “no point masses” is not the full density condition

Every law with a density has P(X=x)=0P(X=x)=0 for each xx. The converse does not hold in full generality: there are singular continuous laws with no atoms and no density with respect to length. The main lecture deliberately confines its continuous calculations to laws admitting PDFs.

A useful boundary case is a mixed law. In a stipulated rainfall model let P(R=0)=0.3P(R=0)=0.3 and, given a positive amount, let RR be uniform on (0,10)(0,10) mm. Then for Borel BB,

P(R∈B)=0.3𝟏{0∈B}+0.07∫B∩(0,10)dr.P(R\in B)=0.3\,\mathbf1_{\{0\in B\}} +0.07\int_{B\cap(0,10)}dr.

The continuous component integrates to 0.70.7; the remaining 0.30.3 is an atom at zero. This law cannot be represented by an ordinary PDF alone. Its CDF is

FR(r)={0,r<0,0.3+0.07r,0≤r<10,1,r≥10.F_R(r)=\begin{cases} 0,&r<0,\\ 0.3+0.07r,&0\le r<10,\\ 1,&r\ge10. \end{cases}

In particular P(R>5)=0.35P(R>5)=0.35. A function that merely equals 0.070.07 on (0,10)(0,10) misses the dry-day mass.

2. CDF properties and endpoints

For any real-valued random variable, FX(x)=P(X≤x)F_X(x)=P(X\le x). If a<ba<b, the disjoint decomposition

{X≤b}={X≤a}∪{a<X≤b}\{X\le b\}=\{X\le a\}\cup\{a<X\le b\}

gives P(a<X≤b)=FX(b)−FX(a)P(a<X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a). Define

FX(a−)=limt↑aFX(t)=P(X<a).F_X(a^-)=\lim_{t\uparrow a}F_X(t)=P(X<a).

Then

P(a≤X≤b)=FX(b)−FX(a−),P(X=a)=FX(a)−FX(a−).P(a\le X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a^-),\qquad P(X=a)=F_X(a)-F_X(a^-).

The continuity properties follow from continuity of probability on monotone sequences of events. For example, {X≤x+1/n}\{X\le x+1/n\} decreases to {X≤x}\{X\le x\}, so FX(x+1/n)→FX(x)F_X(x+1/n)\to F_X(x), establishing right continuity. The events {X≤n}\{X\le n\} increase to Ω\Omega, while {X≤−n}\{X\le-n\} decrease to the empty set; hence the limiting values are 1 and 0.

If a PDF exists, FX(x)=∫−∞xfX(t)dtF_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t)dt is absolutely continuous and FX′=fXF_X'=f_X almost everywhere. An arbitrary CDF need not have this property. In particular, differentiating the rainfall CDF away from zero cannot recover its atom.

3. Marginalization and normalization

Let X,YX,Y be discrete on countable sets SX,SYS_X,S_Y. For fixed xx, the events {X=x,Y=y}\{X=x,Y=y\}, as yy varies, are disjoint and their union agrees with {X=x}\{X=x\} up to a null event. Thus

pX(x)=∑y∈SYpX,Y(x,y).p_X(x)=\sum_{y\in S_Y}p_{X,Y}(x,y).

Summing in xx gives ∑xpX(x)=1\sum_xp_X(x)=1. Nonnegative series may be summed in either order. The corresponding proof applies to pYp_Y.

For a jointly absolutely continuous pair with density fX,Yf_{X,Y}, define

g(x)=∫ℝfX,Y(x,y)dy.g(x)=\int_{\mathbb R}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy.

Tonelli’s theorem permits the iterated integral because the integrand is measurable and nonnegative. For Borel BB,

P(X∈B)=∬B×ℝfX,Y(x,y)dxdy=∫Bg(x)dx.P(X\in B)=\iint_{B\times\mathbb R}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy =\int_B g(x)\,dx.

Therefore gg is a version of the marginal density fXf_X, and ∫g=1\int g=1. A density may be infinite on a null set, but is finite almost everywhere when its integral is 1. These exceptional values can be redefined without changing the law.

The support bounds must be retained. For the main lecture’s maintenance model, integrating over 0<y<x<10<y<x<1 gives fX(x)=2xf_X(x)=2x on (0,1)(0,1) and fY(y)=2(1−y)f_Y(y)=2(1-y) on (0,1)(0,1), both zero elsewhere. Extending the density value 2 across the whole unit square would double its total mass.

4. Conditional distributions and their scope

Discrete conditioning

For pX(x)>0p_X(x)>0,

pY∣X(y∣x)=pX,Y(x,y)pX(x).p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)}{p_X(x)}.

Nonnegativity is inherited from the joint PMF, and

∑ypY∣X(y∣x)=∑ypX,Y(x,y)pX(x)=1.\sum_y p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) =\frac{\sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y)}{p_X(x)}=1.

Multiplying by pX(x)p_X(x) and summing over positive-mass xx gives the law of total probability:

pY(y)=∑x:pX(x)>0pY∣X(y∣x)pX(x).p_Y(y)=\sum_{x:p_X(x)>0}p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)p_X(x).

When pX(x)=0p_X(x)=0, the ratio is undefined. Assignments of a conditional distribution at those values do not affect the joint law or any of the resulting unconditional probabilities.

Conditioning with a joint density

For 0<fX(x)<∞0<f_X(x)<\infty, set

fY∣X(y∣x)=fX,Y(x,y)fX(x).f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}.

For almost every such xx, this is a probability density in yy. It reconstructs joint probabilities through

P(X∈B,Y∈C)=∫B(∫CfY∣X(y∣x)dy)fX(x)dx.P(X\in B,Y\in C) =\int_B\left(\int_C f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,dy\right)f_X(x)\,dx.

This identity is a precise way to characterize a conditional-density version. Values on an XX-null set can be changed without changing the identity. Consequently the law alone does not uniquely prescribe conditional distributions at every individual continuous conditioning value. Smooth textbook formulas choose a natural version.

For a fixed Borel set CC, let gC(u)=∫CfX,Y(u,y)dyg_C(u)=\int_C f_{X,Y}(u,y)dy. If gCg_C and fXf_X are continuous at xx and fX(x)>0f_X(x)>0, then

P(Y∈C∣x−ϵ<X<x+ϵ)=∫x−ϵx+ϵgC(u)du∫x−ϵx+ϵfX(u)du→gC(x)fX(x)=∫CfY∣X(y∣x)dy.\begin{aligned} P(Y\in C\mid x-\epsilon<X<x+\epsilon) &=\frac{\int_{x-\epsilon}^{x+\epsilon}g_C(u)du} {\int_{x-\epsilon}^{x+\epsilon}f_X(u)du}\\ &\longrightarrow\frac{g_C(x)}{f_X(x)} =\int_C f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)dy. \end{aligned}

Divide numerator and denominator by 2ϵ2\epsilon and use continuity to justify the limit. This supports the narrow-band interpretation used for the maintenance model at interior durations. It does not divide an event probability by P(X=x)P(X=x).

For a band BB with P(X∈B)>0P(X\in B)>0, an unambiguous conditional density of YY is

fY∣X∈B(y)=∫BfX,Y(x,y)dxP(X∈B).f_{Y\mid X\in B}(y)= \frac{\int_B f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx}{P(X\in B)}.

This averages over the selected durations with their appropriate weights; it is generally not the conditional density at the band’s midpoint. In the uniform triangular model, the probability of Y<cY<c given a<X<ba<X<b does coincide with the midpoint-conditioned probability when 0<c≤a<b<10<c\le a<b<1. That coincidence for these thresholds does not make the two conditional distributions identical.

5. Independence and factorization

Random variables X,YX,Y are independent if

P(X∈B,Y∈C)=P(X∈B)P(Y∈C)P(X\in B,Y\in C)=P(X\in B)P(Y\in C)

for every pair of Borel sets B,CB,C. In the discrete case, applying this to singleton sets gives the PMF factorization. Conversely, summing a factorized PMF over B×CB\times C proves the definition.

For a pair with a joint density, independence is equivalent to fX,Y=fXfYf_{X,Y}=f_Xf_Y almost everywhere. For the sufficient direction, integrate the product over B×CB\times C and separate the integrals. For the necessary direction, independence makes the joint law the product of the marginal laws, which has density fXfYf_Xf_Y; uniqueness of a density up to null sets gives the result.

A single unequal cell proves dependence in a discrete table. For densities, a discrepancy at one isolated point proves nothing because density values can be changed on null sets. In the maintenance example the discrepancy occupies a rectangle of positive area, so the argument in the main notes establishes dependence.

Continuous marginals need not imply a joint PDF

Let UU be uniform on (0,1)(0,1) and put V=UV=U. Both marginals have uniform densities, but the pair lies on the diagonal v=uv=u with probability 1. That diagonal has plane area zero, so no ordinary two-dimensional PDF can represent this joint law. The main lecture’s joint-PDF formulas assume that a joint density exists, not merely that each marginal has one.

6. Advanced exercises

S1. Mixed rainfall model. For the model in Section 1, calculate P(R=0)P(R=0), P(0<R≤2)P(0<R\le2), P(R≤2)P(R\le2) and P(R>5∣R>0)P(R>5\mid R>0). Explain why differentiating the CDF away from zero loses information.

S2. A narrow-band limit. In the maintenance triangle, take 0<ϵ<1/40<\epsilon<1/4. Calculate P(X>3/4∣1/2−ϵ<Y<1/2+ϵ)P(X>3/4\mid1/2-\epsilon<Y<1/2+\epsilon) by integrating over regions. Take the limit as ϵ↓0\epsilon\downarrow0, derive the natural conditional density of XX given Y=1/2Y=1/2, and verify agreement.

S3. Null-set changes. In the uniform sensor-square model, change the joint density value at (0,0)(0,0) from 1/41/4 to 100. Does the probability law change? Does independence change? Explain using integration.

S4. Continuous marginals without a joint density. For V=UV=U with UU uniform on (0,1)(0,1), calculate P(U≤1/2,V≤1/2)P(U\le1/2,V\le1/2) and compare it with the product of the marginals. Explain both dependence and the absence of a two-dimensional PDF.
