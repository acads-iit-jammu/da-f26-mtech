01.7 supplement · Gaussian structure and conditional laws

Main lecture 01.7

1. Definition including singular laws

A Gaussian vector can be represented as 𝑿=𝝁+A𝒁\mathbf X=\boldsymbol\mu+A\mathbf Z for independent standard normal components of 𝒁\mathbf Z, with covariance Σ=AAT\Sigma=AA^T. The characteristic function is

φ𝑿(t)=E[eitT𝑿]=exp⁡(itT𝝁−12tTΣt).\varphi_{\mathbf X}(t)=E[e^{it^T\mathbf X}] =\exp(it^T\boldsymbol\mu-\tfrac12t^T\Sigma t).

This follows by independence and the standard-normal characteristic function e−t2/2e^{-t^2/2}. It also shows that every linear combination is normal with its corresponding mean and variance. Conversely, the all-linear-combinations definition gives this characteristic function; uniqueness identifies the affine-normal law. A zero variance is allowed in this definition.

If Σ\Sigma is singular, there is nonzero aa with aTΣa=0a^T\Sigma a=0, so aT(𝑿−𝝁)=0a^T(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu)=0 almost surely. The distribution lies in a lower-dimensional affine subspace and has no density with respect to full-dimensional Lebesgue measure. The positive-definite density formula must not be used with a zero determinant.

2. Density and normalization by an invertible map

For positive-definite Σ\Sigma, choose LL invertible with LLT=ΣLL^T=\Sigma, for example its Cholesky factor. The independent-standard-normal density is (2π)−k/2e−𝒛T𝒛/2(2\pi)^{-k/2}e^{-\mathbf z^T\mathbf z/2}. Under 𝒙=𝝁+L𝒛\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\mu+L\mathbf z, the inverse determinant is |det⁡L|−1=|Σ|−1/2|\det L|^{-1}=|\Sigma|^{-1/2}. Substitution gives the stated multivariate density.

Its integral is 1 because the change of variables converts it back to the product of kk normalized standard-normal densities. The covariance is LILT=ΣLIL^T=\Sigma. The factorization also proves that whitening yields independent normal coordinates; identity covariance alone would imply only uncorrelated coordinates for an arbitrary non-Gaussian vector.

3. Linear maps and marginal distributions

For an m×km\times k matrix BB and vector bb, B𝑿+b=B𝝁+b+BA𝒁B\mathbf X+b=B\boldsymbol\mu+b+BA\mathbf Z is Gaussian with covariance BΣBTB\Sigma B^T. Taking BB to select coordinates proves the marginal property. Taking a single row proves the linear-combination property. The characteristic function above establishes that Gaussian laws are determined by their means and covariance matrices, including singular cases.

4. Conditioning in blocks

Partition the vector as (𝑿aT,𝑿bT)T(\mathbf X_a^T,\mathbf X_b^T)^T and partition 𝝁,Σ\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma accordingly. For positive-definite Σ\Sigma, Σaa\Sigma_{aa} is invertible. Define the residual

𝑹=𝑿b−𝝁b−ΣbaΣaa−1(𝑿a−𝝁a).\mathbf R=\mathbf X_b-\boldsymbol\mu_b-\Sigma_{ba}\Sigma_{aa}^{-1}(\mathbf X_a-\boldsymbol\mu_a).

It has mean zero and cross-covariance

Cov⁡(𝑹,𝑿a)=Σba−ΣbaΣaa−1Σaa=0.\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf R,\mathbf X_a)=\Sigma_{ba}-\Sigma_{ba}\Sigma_{aa}^{-1}\Sigma_{aa}=0.

The pair (𝑹,𝑿a)(\mathbf R,\mathbf X_a) is jointly Gaussian. A block-diagonal covariance makes its characteristic function factor into the product of the two block characteristic functions, proving independence. Expansion gives

Cov⁡(𝑹)=Σbb−ΣbaΣaa−1Σab.\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf R)=\Sigma_{bb}-\Sigma_{ba}\Sigma_{aa}^{-1}\Sigma_{ab}.

Consequently a conditional-law version is

𝑿b∣𝑿a=xa∼N(𝝁b+ΣbaΣaa−1(xa−𝝁a),Σbb−ΣbaΣaa−1Σab).\mathbf X_b\mid\mathbf X_a=x_a\sim N\left( \boldsymbol\mu_b+\Sigma_{ba}\Sigma_{aa}^{-1}(x_a-\boldsymbol\mu_a), \Sigma_{bb}-\Sigma_{ba}\Sigma_{aa}^{-1}\Sigma_{ab}\right).

The covariance is the Schur complement and is positive definite here. The bivariate formula follows by scalar substitution. This derivation specifies a continuous density version and does not condition through a ratio with a zero-probability singleton. Conditional distributions are only uniquely determined almost everywhere in general.

The same characteristic-function factorization proves that zero cross-covariance of Gaussian subvectors is equivalent to their independence. The statement requires the joint Gaussian assumption, not merely normal marginal distributions.

5. Probability inside a bivariate ellipse

Let 𝑿∼N2(𝝁,Σ)\mathbf X\sim N_2(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma) with Σ\Sigma positive definite and whiten to 𝒁\mathbf Z. The squared Mahalanobis distance is

Q=(𝑿−𝝁)TΣ−1(𝑿−𝝁)=Z12+Z22.Q=(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu)=Z_1^2+Z_2^2.

For c≥0c\ge0, polar integration gives

P(Q≤c)=∫02π∫0c12πe−r2/2rdrdθ=[−e−r2/2]0c=1−e−c/2.\begin{aligned} P(Q\le c)&=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\sqrt c}\frac1{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2}r\,dr\,d\theta\\ &=\left[-e^{-r^2/2}\right]_0^{\sqrt c}=1-e^{-c/2}. \end{aligned}

Thus QQ has a chi-square distribution with two degrees of freedom. The probability-α\alpha ellipse has c=−2log⁡(1−α)c=-2\log(1-\alpha) for 0<α<10<\alpha<1. In kk dimensions the analogous quadratic form is chi-square with kk degrees of freedom; the simple exponential CDF here is specific to k=2k=2.

6. Transform identities, uniqueness and moment differentiation

For X∼Nk(μ,Σ)X\sim N_k(\mu,\Sigma), allow Σ≽0\Sigma\succeq0. Choose AA with AAT=ΣAA^T=\Sigma and write X=μ+AZX=\mu+AZ with independent standard normal entries of ZZ. For every real tt,

MX(t)=etTμ∏jMZj((ATt)j)=exp⁡(tTμ+12tTΣt).M_X(t)=e^{t^T\mu}\prod_j M_{Z_j}((A^Tt)_j) =\exp(t^T\mu+\tfrac12t^T\Sigma t).

This proves finiteness on all of ℝk\mathbb R^k, including the singular case, without a full-dimensional density. An MGF finite on an open neighborhood of zero determines the distribution. A characteristic function always determines the distribution, with no moment assumptions. The Gaussian characteristic function follows independently from integration by parts for the scalar standard normal and multiplication over independent coordinates, as derived in the main note.

To justify derivatives of the Gaussian MGF, consider a compact set of real parameters tt. A polynomial in XX multiplied by etTXe^{t^TX} is bounded in absolute value by a finite sum of exponentials CesTXC e^{s^TX} for parameters ss in a larger bounded set. Gaussian exponential moments are finite at all these parameters. Dominated convergence therefore justifies differentiation under expectation of every finite order. In particular,

∂i1⋯∂imMX(0)=E[Xi1⋯Xim].\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_m}M_X(0)=E[X_{i_1}\cdots X_{i_m}].

The cumulant generating function is KX(t)=tTμ+tTΣt/2K_X(t)=t^T\mu+t^T\Sigma t/2. Its derivatives above second order vanish. This does not set fourth moments to zero: for a centered Gaussian vector, differentiating etTΣt/2e^{t^T\Sigma t/2} four times gives

E[XiXjXkXl]=ΣijΣkl+ΣikΣjl+ΣilΣjk.E[X_iX_jX_kX_l]=\Sigma_{ij}\Sigma_{kl}+\Sigma_{ik}\Sigma_{jl}+\Sigma_{il}\Sigma_{jk}.

For example E[Xi4]=3Σii2E[X_i^4]=3\Sigma_{ii}^2. This also follows directly by taking the coefficient of ti4t_i^4 in the exponential series. These statements concern centered variables; nonzero means must be included or subtracted first.

7. Block factorization, the Schur complement and singular conditioning

For positive-definite Σ\Sigma, partition into blocks as in the main note. Let B=ΣVUΣUU−1B=\Sigma_{VU}\Sigma_{UU}^{-1} and C=ΣVV−ΣVUΣUU−1ΣUVC=\Sigma_{VV}-\Sigma_{VU}\Sigma_{UU}^{-1}\Sigma_{UV}. For any nonzero vv, take the vector (−ΣUU−1ΣUVv,v)T(-\Sigma_{UU}^{-1}\Sigma_{UV}v,v)^T. Its quadratic form under Σ\Sigma is vTCv>0v^TCv>0, proving that CC is positive definite. Triangular factorization gives

|Σ|=|ΣUU||C|,|\Sigma|=|\Sigma_{UU}||C|,

Σ−1=(ΣUU−1+BTC−1B−BTC−1−C−1BC−1).\Sigma^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_{UU}^{-1}+B^TC^{-1}B&-B^TC^{-1}\\ -C^{-1}B&C^{-1} \end{pmatrix}.

Expanding this quadratic form yields the density factorization in the main note. Integrating the normalized conditional factor proves the marginal density. Dividing by that everywhere-positive marginal yields a conditional-density version for every uu. A conditional distribution is in general specified only almost everywhere; continuity of this formula gives a convenient version throughout ℝr\mathbb R^r.

The residual argument also works if the full covariance is merely positive semidefinite while ΣUU\Sigma_{UU} remains invertible. Then CC is positive semidefinite, and the conditional law may be singular. It is still Gaussian with mean μV+B(u−μU)\mu_V+B(u-\mu_U) and covariance CC, but it need not have an ordinary ss-dimensional density.

If even ΣUU\Sigma_{UU} is singular, use its Moore–Penrose inverse ΣUU+\Sigma_{UU}^{+} and restrict conditioning to the affine support μU+range⁡(ΣUU)\mu_U+\operatorname{range}(\Sigma_{UU}). Positive semidefiniteness ensures that directions with zero variance in UU have zero cross-covariance with VV. Thus a conditional version on that support has

E[V∣U=u]=μV+ΣVUΣUU+(u−μU),E[V\mid U=u]=\mu_V+\Sigma_{VU}\Sigma_{UU}^{+}(u-\mu_U),

Cov⁡(V∣U=u)=ΣVV−ΣVUΣUU+ΣUV.\operatorname{Cov}(V\mid U=u)=\Sigma_{VV}-\Sigma_{VU}\Sigma_{UU}^{+}\Sigma_{UV}.

Values outside the support cannot be inferred from the joint law. This extension is not a license to substitute an ordinary inverse into a singular density formula.

8. Independence, linear maps and conditional residuals

For a jointly Gaussian partition, its characteristic function is

φU,V(s,t)=exp⁡[i(sTμU+tTμV)−12sTΣUUs−sTΣUVt−12tTΣVVt].\varphi_{U,V}(s,t)=\exp\left[i(s^T\mu_U+t^T\mu_V) -\tfrac12s^T\Sigma_{UU}s-s^T\Sigma_{UV}t-\tfrac12t^T\Sigma_{VV}t\right].

If the cross-covariance vanishes this factors as φU(s)φV(t)\varphi_U(s)\varphi_V(t), proving independence by uniqueness. Conversely independence implies zero cross-covariance because second moments exist. The proof includes singular blocks.

For any fixed matrix AA and vector bb,

φAX+b(t)=eitTbφX(ATt)=exp⁡[itT(Aμ+b)−12tTAΣATt].\varphi_{AX+b}(t)=e^{it^Tb}\varphi_X(A^Tt) =\exp\left[it^T(A\mu+b)-\tfrac12t^TA\Sigma A^Tt\right].

This establishes the affine law irrespective of rank. For positive-definite Σ\Sigma, AΣATA\Sigma A^T is positive definite exactly when AA has full row rank: its quadratic form is (ATv)TΣ(ATv)(A^Tv)^T\Sigma(A^Tv).

The conditional residual R=V−μV−B(U−μU)R=V-\mu_V-B(U-\mu_U) is jointly Gaussian with UU and has cross-covariance zero. It is therefore independent of UU, with covariance CC. This yields the conditional law and the identities

E[V]=E[E[V∣U]]=μV,E[V]=E[E[V\mid U]]=\mu_V,

Cov⁡(V)=E[Cov⁡(V∣U)]+Cov⁡(E[V∣U])=C+BΣUUBT=ΣVV.\operatorname{Cov}(V)=E[\operatorname{Cov}(V\mid U)] +\operatorname{Cov}(E[V\mid U])=C+B\Sigma_{UU}B^T=\Sigma_{VV}.

These identities make precise the split between remaining uncertainty and variation explained by the observation. Without joint normality, a residual uncorrelated with the predictors need not be independent of them, and a conditional mean need not be linear.

9. Characteristic-function preview of the central limit theorem

Assume E1,E2,…E_1,E_2,\ldots are independent and identically distributed with finite mean μ\mu and finite variance 0<σ2<∞0<\sigma^2<\infty. Put Zj=(Ej−μ)/σZ_j=(E_j-\mu)/\sigma. No MGF is assumed. Finite E[Z2]E[Z^2] implies finite E|Z|E|Z|, and dominated differentiation gives

φZ′(u)=iE[ZeiuZ],φZ″(u)=−E[Z2eiuZ].\varphi_Z'(u)=iE[Ze^{iuZ}],\qquad \varphi_Z''(u)=-E[Z^2e^{iuZ}].

Dominated convergence makes the second derivative continuous at zero. Since φZ(0)=1\varphi_Z(0)=1, φZ′(0)=0\varphi_Z'(0)=0 and φZ″(0)=−1\varphi_Z''(0)=-1, the second-order Taylor expansion with a little-o remainder is

φZ(u)=1−u2/2+o(u2).\varphi_Z(u)=1-u^2/2+o(u^2).

This argument requires no third moment. For each fixed real tt, independence yields

φSn(t)=[φZ(t/n)]n,Sn=n−1/2∑j=1nZj.\varphi_{S_n}(t)=\left[\varphi_Z(t/\sqrt n)\right]^n, \qquad S_n=n^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^nZ_j.

For t≠0t\ne0, write φZ(t/n)=1+an\varphi_Z(t/\sqrt n)=1+a_n, where an=−t2/(2n)+o(1/n)a_n=-t^2/(2n)+o(1/n). For large nn these numbers lie near 1, where a continuous logarithm is defined. Then

nlog⁡(1+an)=nan+O(n|an|2)→−t2/2.n\log(1+a_n)=na_n+O(n|a_n|^2)\longrightarrow-t^2/2.

At t=0t=0 the characteristic function is identically 1. Hence pointwise convergence holds for every real tt to e−t2/2e^{-t^2/2}.

The continuity theorem states: if characteristic functions converge pointwise to a function continuous at zero, that function is a characteristic function and the corresponding probability laws converge weakly to its law. Conversely, weak convergence implies pointwise convergence of characteristic functions. Applied here, it gives Sn→dN(0,1)S_n\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1). Because the normal CDF is continuous everywhere, P(Sn≤x)→Φ(x)P(S_n\le x)\to\Phi(x) for every real xx.

This is distributional convergence, not convergence of the standardized variable to a fixed number. The unstandardized sample mean converges to μ\mu in probability, while the standardized fluctuations have a nondegenerate limiting law. A finite-sample error bound needs additional analysis and, for common quantitative bounds, stronger moment conditions.

Why a characteristic function adds something beyond an MGF

For a concrete counterexample, let H=eGH=e^G with G∼N(0,1)G\sim N(0,1). Then E[H]=e1/2E[H]=e^{1/2} and E[H2]=e2E[H^2]=e^2, so its variance is finite. Yet for any t>0t>0,

E[etH]=12π∫ℝexp⁡(teg−g2/2)dg=∞.E[e^{tH}]=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb R}\exp(te^g-g^2/2)\,dg=\infty.

Indeed teg−g2/2→∞te^g-g^2/2\to\infty as g→∞g\to\infty, so the integrand is eventually bounded below by a positive constant on an infinite interval. Thus there is no finite MGF on a neighborhood of zero. Its characteristic function still exists, and independent identically distributed copies satisfy the finite-variance central limit theorem after standardization. This example supplies a reason for using characteristic functions beyond the convenience of the Gaussian formulas.
