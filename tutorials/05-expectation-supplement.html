01.5 supplement · Expectation and moment identities

Main lecture 01.5

This supplement states existence conditions and proves the identities used in the worked lecture. Its exercises are unsolved here; solutions are instructor-only.

1. Integrability and induced laws

For a measurable real-valued XX, write X+=max⁡(X,0)X^+=\max(X,0) and X−=max⁡(−X,0)X^-=\max(-X,0). Their nonnegative Lebesgue integrals are always defined in [0,∞][0,\infty]. Define E[X]=E[X+]−E[X−]E[X]=E[X^+]-E[X^-] when the two terms are not both infinite. A finite real mean requires E[|X|]<∞E[|X|]<\infty.

If PXP_X is the induced law, then E[g(X)]=∫gdPXE[g(X)]=\int g\,dP_X whenever g(X)g(X) is nonnegative or integrable. For an indicator g=𝟏Bg=\mathbf1_B, this is precisely P(X∈B)=PX(B)P(X\in B)=P_X(B). Extend by finite linear combinations to nonnegative simple functions, then by monotone convergence to nonnegative measurable functions. Subtract positive and negative parts for integrable functions. This proves the PMF/PDF formulas used in the lecture without requiring an invertible gg.

For a standard Cauchy density f(x)=1/[π(1+x2)]f(x)=1/[\pi(1+x^2)], the positive first-moment integral is proportional to ∫0∞x/(1+x2)dx=∞\int_0^\infty x/(1+x^2)dx=\infty, and the negative part has the same infinite magnitude. The mean is undefined. Symmetric truncated integrals vanish, but this principal-value cancellation is not expectation.

2. Variance and the best constant in squared error

Assume E[X2]<∞E[X^2]<\infty. Cauchy–Schwarz implies E[|X|]≤E[X2]E[|X|]\le\sqrt{E[X^2]}, so the mean exists. Expanding and using linearity,

E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−2μE[X]+μ2=E[X2]−μ2.E[(X-\mu)^2]=E[X^2]-2\mu E[X]+\mu^2=E[X^2]-\mu^2.

For any constant aa,

E[(X−a)2]=Var⁡(X)+(a−μ)2,E[(X-a)^2]=\operatorname{Var}(X)+(a-\mu)^2,

because the cross term contains E[X−μ]=0E[X-\mu]=0. Thus the mean minimizes expected squared error among constant predictions. This does not say the mean minimizes absolute error or other losses.

Since (X−μ)2≥0(X-\mu)^2\ge0, its expectation is zero exactly when it vanishes almost surely. Hence zero variance is equivalent to X=μX=\mu almost surely.

3. Covariance and linear combinations

For finite second moments, Cauchy–Schwarz makes E[|XY|]E[|XY|] finite. Expanding the centered product gives Cov⁡(X,Y)=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y]\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]. Bilinearity then gives

Var⁡(∑iaiXi)=∑i,jaiajCov⁡(Xi,Xj)=aTΣa.\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_i a_iX_i\right)=\sum_{i,j}a_i a_j\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=a^T\Sigma a.

Cauchy–Schwarz applied to centered variables gives |Cov⁡(X,Y)|≤σXσY|\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)|\le\sigma_X\sigma_Y. Correlation is defined only when both variances are positive. Equality corresponds to an almost-sure linear relation between the centered variables. Independence and integrability imply E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]; factorization of this one moment is not a converse criterion for independence.

4. Conditional expectation and total variance

For an integrable XX and a sigma-algebra 𝒢⊆ℱ\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F, E[X∣𝒢]E[X\mid\mathcal G] is a 𝒢\mathcal G-measurable integrable variable mm satisfying ∫AmdP=∫AXdP\int_A m\,dP=\int_A X\,dP for every A∈𝒢A\in\mathcal G. It is unique up to almost-sure equality. Taking A=ΩA=\Omega proves E[m]=E[X]E[m]=E[X]. Conditioning on a variable YY means conditioning on σ(Y)\sigma(Y). PMF and density calculations in the main notes produce versions of this object.

Assume E[X2]<∞E[X^2]<\infty and put m=E[X∣𝒢]m=E[X\mid\mathcal G]. Decompose

X−E[X]=(X−m)+(m−E[X]).X-E[X]=(X-m)+(m-E[X]).

The cross term has expectation zero because E[X−m∣𝒢]=0E[X-m\mid\mathcal G]=0 and m−E[X]m-E[X] is 𝒢\mathcal G-measurable. Therefore

Var⁡(X)=E[(X−m)2]+E[(m−E[X])2]=E[Var⁡(X∣𝒢)]+Var⁡(m).\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-m)^2]+E[(m-E[X])^2] =E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)]+\operatorname{Var}(m).

The same decomposition proves the optimality of mm among square-integrable 𝒢\mathcal G-measurable predictors under squared-error loss.

5. Generating functions and their existence

For a nonnegative integer-valued NN, GN(s)=E[sN]G_N(s)=E[s^N] is defined for 0≤s≤10\le s\le1, using 00=10^0=1 in the series at zero. For 0<s<10<s<1 termwise derivatives follow from power-series theory. As s↑1s\uparrow1, monotone convergence gives GN′(1−)=E[N]G_N'(1^-)=E[N] and GN″(1−)=E[N(N−1)]G_N''(1^-)=E[N(N-1)], with infinite values allowed. Finite second moment yields

Var⁡(N)=GN″(1−)+GN′(1−)−GN′(1−)2.\operatorname{Var}(N)=G_N''(1^-)+G_N'(1^-)-G_N'(1^-)^2.

The moment generating function is MX(t)=E[etX]M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}] where finite. If finite on an open interval containing zero, differentiation under the integral gives MX(k)(0)=E[Xk]M_X^{(k)}(0)=E[X^k] and determines the law uniquely. For a Bernoulli variable, MX(t)=1−p+petM_X(t)=1-p+pe^t, giving first and second raw moments pp. Not every distribution has an MGF finite on such an interval, so one cannot use this method without its existence hypothesis.
