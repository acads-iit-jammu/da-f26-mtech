Purpose and pace

We begin with a six-ticket routing audit and use it to motivate the mathematical objects needed to describe events and assign probabilities. The sequence is:

  1. Compare reviewer and model classifications, including a ticket with two required destinations.
  2. Compare historical interpretations through the audit’s agreement fraction.
  3. Pose Bertrand’s random-chord question and note its different answers under different sampling procedures.
  4. Introduce Kolmogorov’s set-based probability space, then specify uniform selection from the six tickets and define events as sets.
  5. Introduce the triplet’s requirements, a random variable and its law.
  6. Calculate a continuous-time probability by integration.
  7. Practise the same definitions in weighted audits, finance, engineering and training.

The six ticket IDs connect the main argument; the practice problems vary the context. The meeting duration does not set a required pace; stop at a checkpoint and continue at the next meeting when needed.

1. A ticket-routing audit

An IT service desk is checking whether an LLM routes incoming tickets to the appropriate department. The pilot uses three reference outcomes:

  • Billing when Billing is required;
  • Account Support when Account Support is required;
  • Other when neither Billing nor Account Support is required.

“Other” is the complement category for this two-destination question: the reviewer finds that neither Billing nor Account Support is required. Another team may handle such a ticket; “Other” does not mean that the ticket was unreviewed or its status is unknown. A ticket may require both Billing and Account Support, so the reference outcome can contain both labels.

The table is a hypothetical audit. Reviewer labels are operational reference decisions; they are not asserted to be error-free ground truth. For t1t_1, the LLM reports scores sB(x)=0.55s_B(x)=0.55 and sA(x)=0.35s_A(x)=0.35 for Billing and Account Support. These are model scores; calibration as probabilities is not assumed. To obtain the hard model classification shown in the table, apply a threshold of 0.500.50 separately to each destination. The other rows show the resulting model classifications.

Ask:

What does 4/64/6 describe? Under what experiment would it be a probability, and what additional assumptions would connect it to future tickets?

The fraction is an observed summary of these six records. It becomes a probability for selecting uniformly from these six ticket IDs once that selection experiment is specified. It does not, by itself, describe future service-desk traffic.

2. Historical interpretations of probability

Probability has been interpreted in several ways. The accounts below answer different questions about what the number 4/64/6 might mean. None follows just from seeing six rows in a table.

Classical equal-likelihood

The classical rule counts favourable outcomes among equally likely cases. If one ticket ID is selected uniformly from the six IDs, four selections produce agreement, so

P(agreement)=46=23.P(\text{agreement})=\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}.

This calculation is valid for that selection experiment. It does not imply that future tickets are equally likely to be any of these six records, or that the six records represent future traffic. A finite list defines possible cases; the uniform selection rule supplies the equal weights.

The partition chosen for counting also matters. Group the tickets by the pair (reviewer outcome, model classification):

Reviewer outcome Model classification Ticket IDs Exact match?
Billing and Account Support Billing t1t_1 No
Billing only Billing t2,t5t_2,t_5 Yes
Account Support only Account Support t3,t6t_3,t_6 Yes
Other Account Support t4t_4 No

There are four occupied pair types in this audit. If an experiment selected one of these four types uniformly, two of the four would be exact matches, giving 2/42/4. If it selected one of the six ticket IDs uniformly, the pair types would instead have probabilities 1/6,2/6,2/6,1/61/6,2/6,2/6,1/6, giving 4/64/6. Thresholding two destination scores separately permits four model outputs: neither, Billing only, Account Support only, or both. Thus the full product contains 4×4=164\times4=16 reviewer/model pairs, of which four occur in this audit. Uniform selection among all 16 pairs would be a third experiment; its four matching pairs would give agreement probability 4/16=1/44/16=1/4. Equal weighting of pair types does not follow from grouping tickets that way. Counting requires a stated set of cases and a reason to treat those cases as equally likely.

Frequency

The observed agreement proportion is

p̂6=16∑i=16Ii=46,\widehat p_6=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6}I_i=\frac{4}{6},

where Ii=1I_i=1 when the model classification exactly matches the reviewer’s reference outcome for ticket tit_i, and Ii=0I_i=0 otherwise. A frequency interpretation connects a probability to the limiting relative frequency in a specified repeatable process. This audit records one finite set of six tickets; it does not establish that limit or show that future ticket mix and model performance will remain the same.

Repeatedly sampling the LLM for the same text under randomized decoding would define another process. Its output frequency would describe generated classifications, not necessarily agreement with reviewer decisions. The process and event must be stated before interpreting a frequency.

Subjective probability and Bayesian updating

A subjective interpretation treats probability as a person’s degree of belief given available information. An analyst might assess the chance that the model agrees with the review protocol on a future ticket, using this audit and other stated information. The six rows do not determine that assessment uniquely. Coherence constrains probability assignments; subjective does not mean arbitrary. Countable additivity is an additional condition in the Kolmogorov model and does not follow from every finite coherence argument.

Bayesian updating describes how an assessment changes when evidence is observed. If HH is the proposition that the next ticket’s model classification will agree with its reference outcome and DD is additional evidence, Bayes’ rule gives

P(H∣D)=P(D∣H)P(H)P(D),P(D)>0.P(H\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid H)P(H)}{P(D)},\qquad P(D)>0.

The formula does not supply the prior or likelihood; those come from a model and stated information. Conditional probability and Bayesian updating will be developed later.

Logical or evidential probability

A logical or evidential interpretation aims to quantify how strongly stated evidence supports a proposition. Here the evidence could include the six audit rows, the review protocol and assumptions about the future ticket population. A numerical answer requires a rule connecting that evidence and those assumptions to a degree of support. If the rule invokes indifference, it must specify which cases are treated equally and why. Counting ticket IDs and counting occupied label-pair types give different answers because they describe different selection rules. This identifies a problem with an unspecified indifference rule; it does not refute every logical account.

What the interpretations leave for a probability model

Classical probability emphasizes equal likelihood, frequency emphasizes repetition, subjective probability represents an assessment given information, and logical accounts specify evidential support. A propensity account would treat the configured randomized generation process as the chance-producing mechanism. These accounts differ in interpretation. A mathematical calculation also needs the possible outcomes, the event of interest and a probability assignment. The next example shows why the sampling procedure is part of that specification.

3. A second question: Bertrand’s random chord

Draw an equilateral triangle inside a circle. Consider the question:

If a chord is selected at random from the circle, what is the probability that its length exceeds the side length of the triangle?

The phrase “selected at random” does not specify how the chord is generated. Bertrand described different sampling procedures for chords, which give 1/31/3, 1/21/2 and 1/41/4 for the same length comparison. These values follow from different probability distributions induced by the respective sampling procedures; the problem statement alone does not say which procedure to use. John D. Norton’s University of Pittsburgh page presents the three sampling arguments and their values.

The procedures are: choose two independent uniform endpoints (1/31/3); choose a uniform radial distance and an independent uniform direction (1/21/2); or choose a midpoint uniformly in disk area (1/41/4). These are different models. We will not choose among them or derive the answers in this section. The example identifies information a probability question can leave unspecified: the outcomes and, especially, the sampling rule that determines their probabilities.

Kolmogorov’s set-based framework

Kolmogorov’s axiomatic framework gives probability a mathematical foundation in terms of sets and a measure. Its three objects are written

(Ω,ℱ,P).(\Omega,\mathcal F,P).

Here Ω\Omega is the sample space of possible outcomes, ℱ\mathcal F is the collection of events to which probabilities are assigned, and P:ℱ→[0,1]P:\mathcal F\to[0,1] is the probability measure. The event collection has closure requirements, and the measure obeys nonnegativity, normalization and countable additivity. We will define and apply each object using the ticket audit. The supplement returns to Bertrand’s question and specifies the sampling models in full.

4. Define the experiment and its events

To calculate a probability for the audit, specify an experiment:

Select one of the six ticket IDs uniformly at random.

The ticket records, reviewer decisions and model classifications are fixed. The selected ticket ID is the outcome that varies. Thus the sample space is

Ω={t1,t2,t3,t4,t5,t6}.\Omega=\{t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4,t_5,t_6\}.

Define events from the reviewer’s reference classification:

B={t1,t2,t5},A={t1,t3,t6},B=\{t_1,t_2,t_5\},\qquad A=\{t_1,t_3,t_6\},

where BB is the event “Billing is required” and AA is the event “Account Support is required.” The event that neither is required is

O=(B∪A)c={t4}.O=(B\cup A)^c=\{t_4\}.

In this model, OO is precisely the “Other” reference outcome. It is disjoint from BB and AA. The ticket t1t_1 belongs to both BB and AA, because both destinations are required:

B∩A={t1},B∪A={t1,t2,t3,t5,t6}.B\cap A=\{t_1\},\qquad B\cup A=\{t_1,t_2,t_3,t_5,t_6\}.

For sets E,F⊆ΩE,F\subseteq\Omega:

E∩F={t∈Ω:t∈E and t∈F},E∪F={t∈Ω:t∈E or t∈F},Ec=Ω\E.\begin{aligned} E\cap F&=\{t\in\Omega:t\in E\text{ and }t\in F\},\\ E\cup F&=\{t\in\Omega:t\in E\text{ or }t\in F\},\\ E^c&=\Omega\setminus E. \end{aligned}

The “or” in a union is inclusive. The outcome t1t_1 is counted in both marginal events, so to count tickets requiring at least one named destination we use the disjoint pieces B\AB\setminus A, A\BA\setminus B and B∩AB\cap A:

B∪A=(B\A)∪(A\B)∪(B∩A).B\cup A=(B\setminus A)\cup(A\setminus B)\cup(B\cap A).

Under uniform selection, their probabilities are 2/62/6, 2/62/6 and 1/61/6. Therefore

P(B∪A)=26+26+16=56.P(B\cup A)=\frac{2}{6}+\frac{2}{6}+\frac{1}{6}=\frac{5}{6}.

Equivalently,

P(B∪A)=P(B)+P(A)−P(B∩A)=36+36−16=56.P(B\cup A)=P(B)+P(A)-P(B\cap A) =\frac{3}{6}+\frac{3}{6}-\frac{1}{6}=\frac{5}{6}.

The complement is OO, so P(O)=1/6P(O)=1/6. The reviewer outcomes form four possible destination sets: both, Billing only, Account Support only, or neither. “Other” refers only to the last of these in this example.

The exact-agreement event is C={t2,t3,t5,t6}C=\{t_2,t_3,t_5,t_6\}, so P(C)=4/6P(C)=4/6 under this same uniform-ID experiment. We will also represent agreement numerically.

5. The probability space and its requirements

The experiment is represented by the probability triplet

(Ω,ℱ,P).(\Omega,\mathcal F,P).

Object Ticket-audit model Meaning
Ω\Omega {t1,t2,t3,t4,t5,t6}\{t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4,t_5,t_6\} Possible outcomes: ticket IDs that may be selected
ℱ\mathcal F 2Ω2^\Omega Events: all subsets of the six ticket IDs
PP P(E)=|E|/6P(E)=|E|/6 for E⊆ΩE\subseteq\Omega Probability measure under uniform selection

An outcome such as t2t_2 is an element of Ω\Omega. An event such as BB is a subset of Ω\Omega. The measure PP takes an event as input and returns a number in [0,1][0,1]. Here the uniform rule assigns probability 1/61/6 to each singleton outcome.

The event collection ℱ\mathcal F must be a sigma-algebra on Ω\Omega. Its requirements are:

  1. Contains the sample space: Ω∈ℱ\Omega\in\mathcal F.
  2. Closed under complements: if E∈ℱE\in\mathcal F, then Ec=Ω\E∈ℱE^c=\Omega\setminus E\in\mathcal F.
  3. Closed under countable unions: if E1,E2,…∈ℱE_1,E_2,\ldots\in\mathcal F, then ⋃i=1∞Ei∈ℱ\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_i\in\mathcal F.

These conditions ensure that “not EE” and “at least one of E1,E2,…E_1,E_2,\ldots” are events whenever the component sets are events. Since this example has a finite sample space, taking ℱ=2Ω\mathcal F=2^\Omega, the power set, satisfies all three requirements.

The probability measure satisfies Kolmogorov’s axioms:

  1. Nonnegativity: P(E)≥0P(E)\geq0 for each E∈ℱE\in\mathcal F.

  2. Normalization: P(Ω)=1P(\Omega)=1.

  3. Countable additivity: if E1,E2,…E_1,E_2,\ldots are pairwise disjoint events, then

    P(⋃i=1∞Ei)=∑i=1∞P(Ei).P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_i\right) =\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(E_i).

For the six singleton events, additivity gives P(Ω)=6(1/6)=1P(\Omega)=6(1/6)=1. Also BB and BcB^c are disjoint and exhaustive, and P(B)+P(Bc)=3/6+3/6=1P(B)+P(B^c)=3/6+3/6=1. Closure requirements apply to ℱ\mathcal F; the three axioms apply to PP.

A probability measure is a set function from events to the unit interval. The event BB is a set of tickets; P(B)=1/2P(B)=1/2 is a number. This keeps the sample point, event and probability value distinct.

A smaller event collection

Suppose a dashboard records only whether the selected ticket requires Billing. It distinguishes B={t1,t2,t5}B=\{t_1,t_2,t_5\} from Bc={t3,t4,t6}B^c=\{t_3,t_4,t_6\}, but does not identify individual tickets. The events available in this description are

𝒢={⌀,B,Bc,Ω}.\mathcal G=\{\varnothing,B,B^c,\Omega\}.

This is a sigma-algebra: complements exchange the pairs, and any union of its members is again one of the four members. Restrict the uniform-ID measure to 𝒢\mathcal G; the four probabilities are 0,1/2,1/2,10,1/2,1/2,1 respectively. The singleton {t1}\{t_1\} is a subset of Ω\Omega but is not an event in 𝒢\mathcal G. The full audit model still uses ℱ=2Ω\mathcal F=2^\Omega; the smaller model illustrates why the event collection must be specified.

6. A random variable for the same audit

The reviewer event BB can be represented by a numerical function. Define the Billing indicator

XB:Ω→ℝ,XB(t)={1,t∈B,0,t∉B.X_B:\Omega\to\mathbb R,\qquad X_B(t)=\begin{cases} 1,&t\in B,\\ 0,&t\notin B. \end{cases}

Its values are

XB(t1)=1,XB(t2)=1,XB(t3)=0,XB(t4)=0,XB(t5)=1,XB(t6)=0.X_B(t_1)=1,\;X_B(t_2)=1,\;X_B(t_3)=0,\;X_B(t_4)=0, \;X_B(t_5)=1,\;X_B(t_6)=0.

A real-valued random variable is a measurable function from outcomes to real numbers, X:Ω→ℝX:\Omega\to\mathbb R. We explain measurability below using inverse images. Its input is an individual outcome. In contrast, a set function has sets as inputs: the probability measure takes an event EE and returns P(E)P(E). Having a set as its domain does not make every function a set function. Once the outcome is specified, the function’s value is fixed. It is not a random mechanism and it is not the probability measure. In this experiment, the ticket ID is selected at random; XBX_B records whether its reviewer reference includes Billing.

Image: values produced by a set of outcomes

For E⊆ΩE\subseteq\Omega, the image of EE under XBX_B is

XB(E)={XB(t):t∈E}.X_B(E)=\{X_B(t):t\in E\}.

For the Account Support event A={t1,t3,t6}A=\{t_1,t_3,t_6\}, t1t_1 also requires Billing, while t3t_3 and t6t_6 do not. Hence

XB(A)={0,1}.X_B(A)=\{0,1\}.

This is a set of numerical values, not an event in the ticket sample space.

Inverse image: outcomes that produce selected values

For D⊆ℝD\subseteq\mathbb R, the inverse image is

XB−1(D)={t∈Ω:XB(t)∈D}.X_B^{-1}(D)=\{t\in\Omega:X_B(t)\in D\}.

In particular,

XB−1({1})={t1,t2,t5}=B,X_B^{-1}(\{1\})=\{t_1,t_2,t_5\}=B,

and

XB−1({0})={t3,t4,t6}=Bc.X_B^{-1}(\{0\})=\{t_3,t_4,t_6\}=B^c.

An inverse image is a set of outcomes; it need not be an inverse function. The inverse image connects a numerical condition on a random variable to an event to which the probability measure applies:

P(XB=1)=P(XB−1({1}))=P(B)=36=12.P(X_B=1)=P\bigl(X_B^{-1}(\{1\})\bigr)=P(B)=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12.

The distribution of XBX_B is P(XB=1)=1/2P(X_B=1)=1/2 and P(XB=0)=1/2P(X_B=0)=1/2. The function XBX_B, its inverse-image event BB, and the number P(B)P(B) are three different objects.

Measurability and the probability law

To calculate P(XB∈D)P(X_B\in D), the inverse image XB−1(D)X_B^{-1}(D) must be an event. For example, XB−1((1/2,3/2))=B∈ℱX_B^{-1}((1/2,3/2))=B\in\mathcal F, giving probability 1/21/2. This requirement motivates the formal definition.

The Borel sigma-algebra ℬ(ℝ)\mathcal B(\mathbb R) is the smallest sigma-algebra containing all open intervals. It includes intervals, singletons and their countable unions, intersections and complements. A function X:Ω→ℝX:\Omega\to\mathbb R is measurable, and hence a real-valued random variable on (Ω,ℱ,P)(\Omega,\mathcal F,P), if

X−1(D)∈ℱfor every D∈ℬ(ℝ).X^{-1}(D)\in\mathcal F\quad\text{for every }D\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R).

Its law or distribution is the probability measure

PX(D)=P(X−1(D)),D∈ℬ(ℝ).P_X(D)=P(X^{-1}(D)),\qquad D\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R).

In the full finite model every function is measurable because all subsets are events. For XBX_B, the inverse image is one of ⌀,B,Bc,Ω\varnothing,B,B^c,\Omega according to whether DD contains neither of 0 and 1, only 1, only 0, or both. Thus XBX_B is measurable even for the smaller collection 𝒢\mathcal G. Its law assigns mass 1/21/2 each to 0 and 1. By contrast, the ID-code function J(ti)=iJ(t_i)=i is not measurable for 𝒢\mathcal G: J−1({1})={t1}J^{-1}(\{1\})=\{t_1\} is unavailable in that collection. It is measurable for 2Ω2^\Omega.

Also Z−1({2,4,6})={2,4,6}Z^{-1}(\{2,4,6\})=\{2,4,6\}, so Pd(Z is even)=1/2P_d(Z\text{ is even})=1/2. 7. A continuous example: ticket closure time

The triplet is often not written out in routine probability calculations. To see what remains implicit, consider a ticket selected from a specified service process, and let TcT_c be its closure time, measured in hours. The question

What is the probability that this ticket takes more than one day to close?

is written P(Tc>24)P(T_c>24), since one day is 24 hours. In an underlying ticket-system experiment, each outcome ω\omega determines a closure time Tc(ω)T_c(\omega) in hours. The event of interest is the inverse image

Tc−1((24,∞))={ω∈Ω:Tc(ω)>24}.T_c^{-1}((24,\infty))=\{\omega\in\Omega:T_c(\omega)>24\}.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose the closure time follows a Gamma distribution with shape α=2\alpha=2 and scale θ=12\theta=12 hours. These are hypothetical model parameters, not estimates from the six-ticket audit. Under the shape-scale parameterization, the density is

fTc(t)=tα−1e−t/θΓ(α)θα,t≥0.f_{T_c}(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t/\theta}} {\Gamma(\alpha)\theta^\alpha},\qquad t\geq 0.

Here α,θ>0\alpha,\theta>0 and Γ(a)=∫0∞ua−1e−udu\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty u^{a-1}e^{-u}du for a>0a>0; in particular, Γ(2)=1\Gamma(2)=1. With α=2\alpha=2 and θ=12\theta=12 hours, this becomes

fTc(t)=t122e−t/12,t≥0,f_{T_c}(t)=\frac{t}{12^2}e^{-t/12},\qquad t\geq0,

where tt is measured in hours. Here α\alpha is the shape parameter, not the mean. The mean in this shape-scale parameterization is E[Tc]=αθ=24E[T_c]=\alpha\theta=24 hours. The plotted curve shows this model and shades the area to the right of the 24-hour cutoff, which coincides with the mean.

For the six-ticket experiment above, probabilities were calculated by counting equally likely IDs: P(C)=|C|/6P(C)=|C|/6. A continuous ticket time cannot be handled by counting individual possible durations. Under a density model, the probability of an interval is the area under the density over that interval. A density value is not itself a probability; integration accumulates the density across the range. In the audit model, the corresponding finite calculation is a sum of singleton probabilities; for example, P(C)=∑ti∈CP({ti})=4/6P(C)=\sum_{t_i\in C}P(\{t_i\})=4/6. The closure-time probability is the integral of its density over the event. With the substitution u=t/12u=t/12,

P(Tc>24)=∫24∞t144e−t/12dt=∫2∞ue−udu=[−(u+1)e−u]2∞=(2+1)e−2=3e−2≈0.406.\begin{aligned} P(T_c>24) &=\int_{24}^{\infty}\frac{t}{144}e^{-t/12}\,dt\\ &=\int_{2}^{\infty}u e^{-u}\,du\\ &=\left[-(u+1)e^{-u}\right]_2^\infty\\ &=(2+1)e^{-2}=3e^{-2}\approx0.406. \end{aligned}

Thus, under this stipulated model, the probability that closure takes more than 24 hours is about 0.4060.406. A different shape, scale or target ticket population would give a different value. Specifying the Gamma family alone would not suffice; the parameters and their parameterization are needed.

What a probability density specifies

The calculation above uses an area, not a density height. Formally, a random variable TT has a probability density function ff if ff is nonnegative and measurable, ∫ℝf(t)dt=1\int_{\mathbb R}f(t)\,dt=1, and

P(T∈D)=∫Df(t)dtfor every Borel set D.P(T\in D)=\int_D f(t)\,dt\quad\text{for every Borel set }D.

A singleton has zero area, so P(Tc=24)=0P(T_c=24)=0, while a range can have positive probability. Not every random variable has a density; the Billing indicator has a discrete law.

The triplet for the closure-time model

For this calculation, use the following canonical probability space, which models closure time alone:

(Ωc,ℱc,Pc).(\Omega_c,\mathcal F_c,P_c).

In the calculation above, the shorthand P(Tc>24)P(T_c>24) denotes Pc(Tc>24)P_c(T_c>24) on this space. It is distinct from PP, the uniform measure used for selecting one of the six audited ticket IDs.

  1. Sample space: Ωc=[0,∞)\Omega_c=[0,\infty), the possible closure durations measured in hours.

  2. Event collection: ℱc=ℬ([0,∞))\mathcal F_c=\mathcal B([0,\infty)), the Borel subsets of the nonnegative real line: sets obtained by intersecting Borel subsets of ℝ\mathbb R with [0,∞)[0,\infty).

  3. Probability measure: for every E∈ℱcE\in\mathcal F_c,

    Pc(E)=∫EfTc(t)dt,fTc(t)=t144e−t/12,t≥0.P_c(E)=\int_E f_{T_c}(t)\,dt, \qquad f_{T_c}(t)=\frac{t}{144}e^{-t/12},\quad t\geq0.

This is one explicit probability space realizing the assumed Gamma law. A full ticket-system model could use outcomes that also include ticket type, priority and other information, with closure time as a function on that larger space. In routine calculations, Tc∼Gamma⁡(shape 2,scale 12 h)T_c\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\text{shape }2,\text{scale }12\text{ h}) states the induced law needed for probabilities about closure time without writing the whole triplet. The distribution of TcT_c does not by itself describe every other random quantity in the ticket system.

This is why many engineering and AI calculations work directly with random variables and their distributions after the probability-space foundation has been established.

9. Connection to AI evaluation and continuation

The probability 2/32/3 here concerns uniform selection from six audited ticket IDs. It is not automatically the model’s agreement probability on future traffic. That requires a defined target population, an appropriate sampling process and assumptions connecting this audit to future tickets. Changing the reviewer protocol may also change the event called “agreement.”

The same distinctions arise in AI evaluation. A test-set accuracy is an observed proportion. Interpreting it as a probability of correct classification for future cases requires additional assumptions. If model generation is stochastic, its repeated-output distribution is another probability model with a different outcome space. In each case, specify the outcomes, event and sampling or generation process before interpreting a probability.

A suitable first stopping point is the calculation of P(B∪A)=5/6P(B\cup A)=5/6 at the end of Section 4. Continue to the triplet, sigma-algebra and axioms once the class can identify the complement event OO. The random-variable and image sections can continue in a later meeting. Bertrand’s chord problem is posed here but not derived; the rigorous supplement specifies its sampling models and works through their parametrizations and probabilities.
