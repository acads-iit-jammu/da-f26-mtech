01.5 · Expectation, mean and variance

Data Analytics for AI · Executive M.Tech · IIT Jammu
Previous: meeting problem · Rigorous supplement · Next: transformations

Questions and objectives

A service team needs to budget reviewer time. A reliability engineer needs to distinguish consistent performance from occasional large deviations. An evaluation lead needs to combine results from task groups with different frequencies. A distribution answers probability questions; expectation summarizes specified numerical functions of that distribution.

We will define expectation for discrete and continuous variables, identify the mean and variance, calculate expectations of functions, and use covariance and conditional expectation. All datasets and application models are hypothetical. Worked examples include solutions; practice checks are for independent work.

1. Begin with a weighted workload

Worked example: reviewer time per request

Select one request under a stipulated model. Let XX be the additional review time in minutes. The possible values and probabilities are:

xx (minutes) 0 2 5
pX(x)p_X(x) 0.5 0.3 0.2
xpX(x)x p_X(x) 0 0.6 1.0

For planning, weight each possible time by its probability:

0(0.5)+2(0.3)+5(0.2)=1.6 minutes per request.0(0.5)+2(0.3)+5(0.2)=1.6\text{ minutes per request}.

This is not a claim that any request takes 1.6 minutes. In this model no request does. It is a probability-weighted average. If 100 requests each have this marginal distribution, their expected total additional review time is 100(1.6)=160100(1.6)=160 minutes. Their realized total can differ, and independence has not yet been assumed.

Definition: expectation and mean

Let XX be a real random variable on (Ω,ℱ,P)(\Omega,\mathcal F,P). Its expectation, also called its mean, is

μX=E[X]=∫ΩX(ω)dP(ω),\mu_X=E[X]=\int_\Omega X(\omega)\,dP(\omega),

when E[|X|]<∞E[|X|]<\infty. For calculations using the induced distribution,

E[X]=∑xxpX(x)(discrete),E[X]=∫−∞∞xfX(x)dx(a density exists).E[X]=\sum_x x p_X(x)\quad\text{(discrete)},\qquad E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx\quad\text{(a density exists)}.

The sum or integral must converge absolutely for a finite expectation. A nonnegative variable may have expectation +∞+\infty. For signed variables, cancellation of divergent positive and negative contributions does not define a mean. The supplement gives a boundary example.

The units of the mean are the units of XX. In the workload example, E[X]=1.6E[X]=1.6 minutes. The PMF supplies the weights; it is not an ordinary average of the distinct possible values, which would incorrectly give (0+2+5)/3(0+2+5)/3.

A sample mean X‾n=(X1+⋯+Xn)/n\bar X_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n is a statistic computed from observations; the model mean μX\mu_X is a property of a probability law. For independent, identically distributed observations with finite absolute mean, the law of large numbers gives a long-run interpretation. A small sample need not have an average near μX\mu_X; convergence is developed in Module 02.

2. Continuous expectation

Worked example: the gap between the friends’ arrivals

In the independent uniform-arrival model from 01.4, the gap D=|TA−TB|D=|T_A-T_B| has density fD(d)=2(1−d)f_D(d)=2(1-d) for 0<d<10<d<1, zero elsewhere. What is the mean gap?

E[D]=∫01d2(1−d)dd=2∫01(d−d2)dd=2[d22−d33]01=13 hour=20 minutes.\begin{aligned} E[D]&=\int_0^1 d\,2(1-d)\,dd =2\int_0^1(d-d^2)\,dd\\ &=2\left[\frac{d^2}{2}-\frac{d^3}{3}\right]_0^1 =\frac13\text{ hour}=20\text{ minutes}. \end{aligned}

The integrand is value times density. Density alone integrates to probability; multiplying by the value before integrating gives its probability-weighted mean. This is the mean arrival gap, not the first friend’s actual waiting time, which is capped at 15 minutes by the policy.

Worked example: actual waiting time

The fully worked meeting-problem appendix derives the complete CDF, its jump, and both moments step by step.

Let W=min⁡(D,1/4)W=\min(D,1/4) be the time the first arrival spends waiting. If the gap is below 1/41/4, the wait equals the gap; otherwise it is 1/41/4 even though the friends fail to meet. Then

E[W]=∫01/4d2(1−d)dd+14∫1/412(1−d)dd=596+14916=37192 hour=11.5625 minutes.\begin{aligned} E[W] &=\int_0^{1/4}d\,2(1-d)\,dd +\frac14\int_{1/4}^1 2(1-d)\,dd\\ &=\frac5{96}+\frac14\frac9{16} =\frac{37}{192}\text{ hour} =11.5625\text{ minutes}. \end{aligned}

This calculation motivates the expectation-of-a-function rule (often called LOTUS). For an integrable measurable g(X)g(X),

E[g(X)]=∑xg(x)pX(x)quadorE[g(X)]=∫g(x)fX(x)dx.E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p_X(x)quad\text{or}\quad E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f_X(x)\,dx.

We do not need to derive the distribution of g(X)g(X) first. In fact WW has a point mass at 1/41/4, so treating it as having only a smooth PDF would miss part of its law.

For a pair with a joint density, the corresponding formula is

E[g(X,Y)]=∬g(x,y)fX,Y(x,y)dxdy,E[g(X,Y)]=\iint g(x,y)f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy,

provided the same integral with |g||g| is finite. Replace the double integral by a double sum for a joint PMF.

3. Variance: distinguish means from variability

Same mean, different performance

Suppose system A always takes two seconds, while system B takes one or three seconds with equal probability. Both means are two seconds. Their mean signed deviations are zero, so signed deviations cannot measure spread. Squaring the deviations gives 0 for A and 1 square second for B.

For a variable with finite second moment, the variance and standard deviation are

Var⁡(X)=E[(X−μX)2],SD⁡(X)=Var⁡(X).\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu_X)^2],\qquad \operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}.

Variance is nonnegative and has squared units; standard deviation has the original units. Expanding the square gives the computational identity

Var⁡(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2.\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2.

The mean is the first raw moment; E[X2]E[X^2] is the second raw moment; variance is the second moment about the mean. A zero variance means X=μXX=\mu_X with probability 1.

Worked example: reviewer-time variance

For the initial PMF,

E[X2]=02(0.5)+22(0.3)+52(0.2)=6.2.E[X^2]=0^2(0.5)+2^2(0.3)+5^2(0.2)=6.2.

Therefore

Var⁡(X)=6.2−1.62=3.64 minutes2,SD⁡(X)=3.64≈1.908 minutes.\operatorname{Var}(X)=6.2-1.6^2=3.64\text{ minutes}^2, \qquad\operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{3.64}\approx1.908\text{ minutes}.

Check directly:

0.5(0−1.6)2+0.3(2−1.6)2+0.2(5−1.6)2=1.28+0.048+2.312=3.64.0.5(0-1.6)^2+0.3(2-1.6)^2+0.2(5-1.6)^2 =1.28+0.048+2.312=3.64.

Variance summarizes spread about the mean. It does not by itself specify a tail probability or guarantee a service deadline.

Worked example: continuous gap variance

For the arrival gap,

E[D2]=2∫01(d2−d3)dd=2(13−14)=16.E[D^2]=2\int_0^1(d^2-d^3)\,dd =2\left(\frac13-\frac14\right)=\frac16.

Thus

Var⁡(D)=16−19=118 hour2.\operatorname{Var}(D)=\frac16-\frac19=\frac1{18}\text{ hour}^2.

If Dm=60DD_m=60D is the gap in minutes, its mean is 20 minutes and its variance is 602/18=20060^2/18=200 minutes squared. Its standard deviation is 200≈14.142\sqrt{200}\approx14.142 minutes.

4. Linear combinations and covariance

Convert a workload into a cost

If a processing charge is C=10+3XC=10+3X rupees, with XX measured in minutes, then the initial workload model gives

E[C]=10+3(1.6)=14.8 rupees,Var⁡(C)=32(3.64)=32.76 rupees2.E[C]=10+3(1.6)=14.8\text{ rupees},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(C)=3^2(3.64)=32.76\text{ rupees}^2.

In general, for integrable variables,

E[aX+bY+c]=aE[X]+bE[Y]+c.E[aX+bY+c]=aE[X]+bE[Y]+c.

Linearity needs no independence. For finite second moments, Var⁡(aX+b)=a2Var⁡(X)\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X). Adding a fixed amount shifts the mean but does not change spread.

Why two duration variances may not add

In the triangular support-ticket model, fX,Y(x,y)=1/2f_{X,Y}(x,y)=1/2 on 0<y<x<20<y<x<2. The marginals are fX(x)=x/2f_X(x)=x/2 and fY(y)=(2−y)/2f_Y(y)=(2-y)/2 on (0,2)(0,2). Direct integration gives

E[X]=∫02x22dx=43,E[Y]=∫02y(2−y)2dy=23,E[X]=\int_0^2\frac{x^2}{2}\,dx=\frac43,\quad E[Y]=\int_0^2\frac{y(2-y)}2\,dy=\frac23,

E[X2]=2,E[Y2]=23,E[X^2]=2,\qquad E[Y^2]=\frac23,

and hence both variances are 2/92/9. For the total duration, we also need

E[XY]=∫02∫0xxy2dydx=∫02x34dx=1.E[XY]=\int_0^2\int_0^x\frac{xy}{2}\,dy\,dx =\int_0^2\frac{x^3}{4}\,dx=1.

The covariance is

Cov⁡(X,Y)=E[(X−E[X])(Y−E[Y])]=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y].\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y].

Here it is 1−(4/3)(2/3)=1/91-(4/3)(2/3)=1/9 hours squared. Expanding a squared sum gives

Var⁡(aX+bY)=a2Var⁡(X)+b2Var⁡(Y)+2abCov⁡(X,Y).\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)+b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).

For T=X+YT=X+Y, E[T]=2E[T]=2 hours and

Var⁡(T)=29+29+219=23 hours2.\operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac29+\frac29+2\frac19=\frac23\text{ hours}^2.

Omitting covariance would give 4/94/9, an incorrect result for this model. When both variances are positive, correlation is

ρX,Y=Cov⁡(X,Y)SD⁡(X)SD⁡(Y);\rho_{X,Y}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\operatorname{SD}(X)\operatorname{SD}(Y)};

here it is 1/21/2. Correlation is dimensionless and lies in [−1,1][-1,1]. Independence implies zero covariance when second moments exist; the converse generally fails.

Worked distinction: uncorrelated does not mean independent

Let UU be uniform on [−1,1][-1,1] and V=U2V=U^2. Symmetry gives E[U]=E[U3]=0E[U]=E[U^3]=0, so Cov⁡(U,V)=0\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)=0. Yet VV is determined by UU. More explicitly, the events |U|≤1/2|U|\le1/2 and V≤1/4V\le1/4 are identical, each with probability 1/21/2; their intersection has probability 1/21/2, not 1/41/4.

5. Conditional means and changing task populations

Worked example: two task groups

Let GG denote the type of a randomly selected task. Stipulate P(G=routine)=0.75P(G=\mathrm{routine})=0.75 and P(G=complex)=0.25P(G=\mathrm{complex})=0.25. The review-time means within these groups are 2 and 6 minutes; their variances are 1 and 4 minutes squared.

Weight the group means by group probabilities:

E[X]=0.75(2)+0.25(6)=3 minutes.E[X]=0.75(2)+0.25(6)=3\text{ minutes}.

A conditional mean is the expectation under a conditional distribution:

m(g)=E[X∣G=g]=∑xxpX∣G(x∣g)m(g)=E[X\mid G=g]=\sum_x x p_{X\mid G}(x\mid g)

or its density integral, where defined. The random variable m(G)=E[X∣G]m(G)=E[X\mid G] assigns each outcome its group’s mean. The law of total expectation is

E[X]=E[E[X∣G]].E[X]=E[E[X\mid G]].

For finite second moments, the law of total variance is

Var⁡(X)=E[Var⁡(X∣G)]+Var⁡(E[X∣G]).\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid G)]+\operatorname{Var}(E[X\mid G]).

Here the within-group term is 0.75(1)+0.25(4)=1.750.75(1)+0.25(4)=1.75. The between-group term is

0.75(2−3)2+0.25(6−3)2=0.75+2.25=3.0.75(2-3)^2+0.25(6-3)^2=0.75+2.25=3.

The total variance is 4.754.75 minutes squared. Averaging the conditional variances alone would miss variability due to the different group means. A change in task proportions can change the overall mean and variance even when each group’s performance is unchanged.

Continuous conditioning in the support-ticket triangle

The conditional density of YY given X=xX=x is 1/x1/x on (0,x)(0,x), for 0<x<20<x<2. Consequently,

E[Y∣X=x]=∫0xy1xdy=x2.E[Y\mid X=x]=\int_0^x y\frac1x\,dy=\frac x2.

Using the marginal of XX,

E[E[Y∣X]]=∫02x2x2dx=23,E[E[Y\mid X]]=\int_0^2\frac x2\frac x2\,dx=\frac23,

which agrees with the direct calculation of E[Y]E[Y]. This is conditioning through a density version, not division by P(X=x)=0P(X=x)=0.

6. Expectations of indicators and generating functions

A review passes with stipulated probability pp. Let I=1I=1 for a pass and 0 otherwise. Then

E[I]=0(1−p)+1p=p,Var⁡(I)=p−p2=p(1−p).E[I]=0(1-p)+1p=p,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(I)=p-p^2=p(1-p).

This gives the general identity E[𝟏A]=P(A)E[\mathbf1_A]=P(A). A sum of indicators counts occurrences, so its expected count is the sum of the occurrence probabilities, even when the indicators are dependent.

For a nonnegative integer-valued count NN, the probability generating function is GN(s)=E[sN]=∑n≥0pN(n)snG_N(s)=E[s^N]=\sum_{n\ge0}p_N(n)s^n for 0≤s≤10\le s\le1. For the reviewer-count PMF P(N=0,1,2)=(0.5,0.3,0.2)P(N=0,1,2)=(0.5,0.3,0.2),

GN(s)=0.5+0.3s+0.2s2,GN′(1)=0.7=E[N].G_N(s)=0.5+0.3s+0.2s^2,\qquad G_N'(1)=0.7=E[N].

Also GN″(1)=0.4=E[N(N−1)]G_N''(1)=0.4=E[N(N-1)], so Var⁡(N)=0.4+0.7−0.72=0.61\operatorname{Var}(N)=0.4+0.7-0.7^2=0.61. For infinite supports, derivatives at 1 are understood as left limits with the relevant finite-moment assumptions. The supplement also defines the moment generating function; it need not exist near zero for every law.
