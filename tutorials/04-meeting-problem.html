Appendix A: complete waiting-time CDF, mixed law, mean and variance · CDF and density sliders

1. The question and the experiment

Two friends agree to meet at a reference time t=0t=0. Each arrives during the following hour. Whoever arrives first waits for 15 minutes, then leaves if the other has not arrived. What is the probability that they do not meet?

This student-raised problem lets us distinguish a set of outcomes from the probability assigned to it. We will solve the same question under two stipulated arrival models: independent uniform arrivals, and independent arrivals with a density that decreases over the hour. Neither is claimed to describe observed arrival behavior.

We assume each friend does arrive within the hour, the first arrival waits the full 15 minutes even if that extends beyond the hour, and arrival exactly at the departure time counts as a meeting. The boundary convention does not affect probabilities under the two density models below.

Identify the probability objects

Measure time in hours. An outcome is the ordered pair ω=(tA,tB)\omega=(t_A,t_B): A’s arrival time first, B’s second. For example, (0.2,0.3)(0.2,0.3) means A arrives after 12 minutes and B after 18 minutes; they meet because the gap is six minutes.

Take

Ω=[0,1]2,ℱ=ℬ([0,1]2).\Omega=[0,1]^2,\qquad \mathcal F=\mathcal B([0,1]^2).

The Borel sigma-algebra includes the rectangles, triangles and diagonal bands used here. Define the coordinate random variables

TA(tA,tB)=tA,TB(tA,tB)=tB.T_A(t_A,t_B)=t_A,\qquad T_B(t_A,t_B)=t_B.

The triplet is (Ω,ℱ,P)(\Omega,\mathcal F,P), but the specification is incomplete until we choose PP. A joint density will assign probabilities to events by integration.

2. Describe the event before choosing a density

The waiting allowance is δ=15/60=1/4\delta=15/60=1/4 hour. The friends meet precisely when their arrival-time difference is at most δ\delta:

Mδ={ω:|TA(ω)−TB(ω)|≤δ}.M_\delta=\{\omega:|T_A(\omega)-T_B(\omega)|\le\delta\}.

Its complement, the event asked for, is

Nδ={ω:|TA(ω)−TB(ω)|>δ}.N_\delta=\{\omega:|T_A(\omega)-T_B(\omega)|>\delta\}.

Split the no-meeting event into two disjoint possibilities:

Nδ={TB>TA+δ}∪{TA>TB+δ}.N_\delta=\{T_B>T_A+\delta\}\ \cup\ \{T_A>T_B+\delta\}.

In the first, B arrives too late for A; in the second, A arrives too late for B. On the unit square with tAt_A horizontal and tBt_B vertical, the meeting event is the band between tB=tA−δt_B=t_A-\delta and tB=tA+δt_B=t_A+\delta. The two remaining corner triangles form NδN_\delta. These are measurable regions, so PP can act on them.

Before calculating: sketch the unit square and locate (0.2,0.3)(0.2,0.3) and (0.1,0.8)(0.1,0.8). Decide which belongs to the meeting band. The model has not yet told us whether area alone gives probability.

3. Model U: independent uniform arrivals

Specify the measure

Assume TAT_A and TBT_B are independent and each uniform on [0,1][0,1]. Their marginal densities are 1 on that interval, zero elsewhere. Independence gives

fU(tA,tB)={1,(tA,tB)∈[0,1]2,0,otherwise.f_U(t_A,t_B)=\begin{cases}1,&(t_A,t_B)\in[0,1]^2,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For a Borel event E⊆ΩE\subseteq\Omega,

PU(E)=∬E1dtAdtB.P_U(E)=\iint_E1\,dt_A\,dt_B.

This integrates to 1 on the square. Under this particular measure, probability equals area. Merely specifying the square would not justify equal area weights.

Geometry first: probability of not meeting

At δ=1/4\delta=1/4, each no-meeting triangle has two perpendicular sides of length 1−δ=3/41-\delta=3/4. Its area is

12(34)2=932.\frac12\left(\frac34\right)^2=\frac9{32}.

The triangles are disjoint, so

PU(N1/4)=2(932)=916=0.5625.P_U(N_{1/4})=2\left(\frac9{32}\right)=\boxed{\frac9{16}=0.5625}.

Consequently,

PU(M1/4)=1−916=716=0.4375.P_U(M_{1/4})=1-\frac9{16}=\boxed{\frac7{16}=0.4375}.

The answer to the original no-meeting question is 56.25% under independent uniform arrivals.

Recover the result by a double integral

For the upper triangle, 0≤tA<3/40\le t_A<3/4 and tA+1/4<tB≤1t_A+1/4<t_B\le1. Symmetry of this joint density under exchanging the friends gives equal probabilities to the two triangles:

PU(N1/4)=2∫03/4∫tA+1/411dtBdtA=2∫03/4(34−tA)dtA=2[34tA−tA22]03/4=916.\begin{aligned} P_U(N_{1/4}) &=2\int_0^{3/4}\int_{t_A+1/4}^1 1\,dt_B\,dt_A\\ &=2\int_0^{3/4}\left(\frac34-t_A\right)dt_A\\ &=2\left[\frac34t_A-\frac{t_A^2}{2}\right]_0^{3/4} =\frac9{16}. \end{aligned}

Integration adds density over the original event, just as a finite probability model adds outcome weights. The factor of 2 uses symmetry; independence alone would not justify it if the two arrival distributions differed.

Suggested stopping point. Have learners explain the integration bounds and why the two triangles have equal probabilities before changing the arrival model.

4. Model E: independent arrivals weighted toward earlier times

Normalize the proposed marginal density

Keep the same experiment and waiting rule. Now stipulate that each arrival has density proportional to 1−t1-t on [0,1][0,1]. With fT(t)=c(1−t)f_T(t)=c(1-t) there,

1=∫01c(1−t)dt=c[t−t22]01=c2,1=\int_0^1c(1-t)\,dt=c\left[t-\frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^1=\frac c2,

so

fT(t)={2(1−t),0≤t≤1,0,otherwise.f_T(t)=\begin{cases}2(1-t),&0\le t\le1,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This is a different hypothetical model, not a conclusion that these friends actually arrive early. A higher density near zero means an interval of fixed small length near zero receives more probability than one near the end of the hour. A single exact time still has probability zero.

For example,

PE(TA≤1/4)=∫01/42(1−t)dt=[2t−t2]01/4=716,P_E(T_A\le1/4)=\int_0^{1/4}2(1-t)\,dt =\left[2t-t^2\right]_0^{1/4}=\frac7{16},

compared with 1/41/4 in Model U.

Independence still holds, but the measure changes

Assume the two arrivals are independent with this same marginal density. The joint density is

fE(tA,tB)=4(1−tA)(1−tB)on [0,1]2,f_E(t_A,t_B)=4(1-t_A)(1-t_B)\quad\text{on }[0,1]^2,

and zero elsewhere. It integrates to 1 because each marginal integrates to 1. The probability measure is now

PE(E)=∬E4(1−tA)(1−tB)dtAdtB.P_E(E)=\iint_E4(1-t_A)(1-t_B)\,dt_A\,dt_B.

The outcome space, sigma-algebra, coordinate functions and meeting event are unchanged. Only the probability measure changes. Probability is no longer the unweighted area of a region.

Integrate over the same two triangles

The density remains symmetric in tA,tBt_A,t_B. Hence

PE(N1/4)=2∫03/4∫tA+1/414(1−tA)(1−tB)dtBdtA.P_E(N_{1/4})=2\int_0^{3/4}\int_{t_A+1/4}^1 4(1-t_A)(1-t_B)\,dt_B\,dt_A.

For a fixed tAt_A,

∫tA+1/41(1−tB)dtB=12(34−tA)2.\int_{t_A+1/4}^1(1-t_B)\,dt_B =\frac12\left(\frac34-t_A\right)^2.

Thus

PE(N1/4)=4∫03/4(1−tA)(34−tA)2dtA.P_E(N_{1/4})=4\int_0^{3/4}(1-t_A)\left(\frac34-t_A\right)^2dt_A.

To show the remaining arithmetic, put u=3/4−tAu=3/4-t_A, so 1−tA=1/4+u1-t_A=1/4+u:

PE(N1/4)=4∫03/4(14+u)u2du=4[u312+u44]03/4=964+81256=117256≈0.45703125.\begin{aligned} P_E(N_{1/4}) &=4\int_0^{3/4}\left(\frac14+u\right)u^2\,du\\ &=4\left[\frac{u^3}{12}+\frac{u^4}{4}\right]_0^{3/4}\\ &=\frac9{64}+\frac{81}{256} =\boxed{\frac{117}{256}\approx0.45703125}. \end{aligned}

Therefore

PE(M1/4)=1−117256=139256≈0.54296875.P_E(M_{1/4})=1-\frac{117}{256}=\boxed{\frac{139}{256}\approx0.54296875}.

Arrival model Meet within the waiting allowance Do not meet
Independent uniform 7/16=43.75%7/16=43.75\% 9/16=56.25%9/16=56.25\%
Independent decreasing-density 139/256≈54.30%139/256\approx54.30\% 117/256≈45.70%117/256\approx45.70\%

Both models assume independent arrivals. In this comparison, changing both marginal densities increases the probability that the two times are close. Independence does not fix the meeting probability. Nor does the calculation prove that every possible model favoring early arrivals must improve it.

5. Transform the pair into an arrival-time gap

A one-dimensional variable for the same question

Define the measurable function g(tA,tB)=|tA−tB|g(t_A,t_B)=|t_A-t_B| and the random variable

D=g(TA,TB)=|TA−TB|.D=g(T_A,T_B)=|T_A-T_B|.

Its values are gaps in hours, from 0 to 1. The meeting event is the inverse image

Mδ=D−1([0,δ])={D≤δ}.M_\delta=D^{-1}([0,\delta])=\{D\le\delta\}.

Many arrival pairs have the same gap. Thus the distribution of DD retains the gap information and discards the individual arrival times. It is induced by the original measure: for a Borel set BB of gaps, PD(B)=P(D−1(B))P_D(B)=P(D^{-1}(B)).

Derive the gap CDF and PDF under uniform arrivals

For 0≤d≤10\le d\le1, the two triangles outside the band of width dd have total area (1−d)2(1-d)^2. Hence the CDF, defined by FD,U(d)=PU(D≤d)F_{D,U}(d)=P_U(D\le d), is

FD,U(d)={0,d<0,1−(1−d)2=2d−d2,0≤d≤1,1,d>1.F_{D,U}(d)=\begin{cases} 0,&d<0,\\ 1-(1-d)^2=2d-d^2,&0\le d\le1,\\ 1,&d>1. \end{cases}

Differentiating on (0,1)(0,1) gives a PDF:

fD,U(d)=2(1−d)(0<d<1),f_{D,U}(d)=2(1-d)\quad(0<d<1),

zero outside [0,1][0,1]. Its values at the endpoints do not affect any probability. It is nonnegative and integrates to 1. Recover the meeting probability:

PU(D≤1/4)=∫01/42(1−d)dd=[2d−d2]01/4=716.P_U(D\le1/4)=\int_0^{1/4}2(1-d)\,dd =\left[2d-d^2\right]_0^{1/4}=\frac7{16}.

This density has the same formula as the individual arrival-time density in Model E, but it describes a different variable under a different probability measure. In Model E, the gap density is not 2(1−d)2(1-d).

6. Vary the waiting policy, retaining the models

Let ww be the waiting allowance in minutes and δ=w/60\delta=w/60. For 0≤δ≤10\le\delta\le1, the uniform calculation gives

PU(Nδ)=(1−δ)2,PU(Mδ)=2δ−δ2.P_U(N_\delta)=(1-\delta)^2,\qquad P_U(M_\delta)=2\delta-\delta^2.

In Model E, the same integration with 1/41/4 replaced by δ\delta gives, with a=1−δa=1-\delta,

PE(Nδ)=4∫01−δ(1−t)(1−δ−t)2dt=4∫0a(δ+u)u2du=4δa33+a4=(1−δ)3(1+δ3).\begin{aligned} P_E(N_\delta) &=4\int_0^{1-\delta}(1-t)(1-\delta-t)^2\,dt\\ &=4\int_0^a(\delta+u)u^2\,du =\frac{4\delta a^3}{3}+a^4\\ &=(1-\delta)^3\left(1+\frac\delta3\right). \end{aligned}

Therefore

PE(Mδ)=1−(1−δ)3(1+δ3).P_E(M_\delta)=1-(1-\delta)^3\left(1+\frac\delta3\right).

At zero waiting time, meeting requires equal arrival times, a diagonal of probability zero under both models. At 60 minutes, all pairs qualify and the meeting probability is 1. Increasing the waiting allowance enlarges the event without changing either model’s measure.

Appendix A · From joint arrivals to the actual waiting-time distribution

A.1. Questions, assumptions and notation

If the friends arrive 40 minutes apart, the first friend waits only 15 minutes before leaving. The arrival gap and the actual waiting time therefore answer different questions. In this appendix we derive the distributions of both, then calculate the mean and variance of the actual wait.

The learning sequence is to construct the joint density, define a function of the pair, calculate its CDF from an event region, differentiate when a density exists, apply a second transformation, identify any resulting point mass, and calculate moments using the complete law.

We use Model U only: the two arrivals are independent and uniform during the hour following t=0t=0. Each friend does arrive within that hour. The first friend waits up to 15 minutes even if that extends beyond the end of the arrival window. Arrival exactly at the departure time counts as a meeting; this equality has probability zero in the stipulated model.

To remain consistent with the rest of this course, we write

D=|TA−TB|for the arrival gap,W=min⁡(D,1/4)for the actual wait.D=|T_A-T_B|\quad\text{for the arrival gap},\qquad W=\min(D,1/4)\quad\text{for the actual wait}.

All formula arguments below are in hours, unless stated otherwise. WW records the wait of whichever friend arrives first, not the wait of a particular named friend or the sum of both friends’ waits. At a simultaneous arrival, define the wait as zero.

Open the interactive CDF, density and point-mass demonstration. Start at a 15-minute cap and compare the CDF immediately below the cap with its value at the cap. Then change the waiting policy.

A.2. Construct the probability triplet and joint law

An outcome is an ordered pair ω=(tA,tB)\omega=(t_A,t_B). The probability model is

Ω=[0,1]2,ℱ=ℬ([0,1]2),PU(E)=∬E1dtAdtB(E∈ℱ).\Omega=[0,1]^2,\qquad\mathcal F=\mathcal B([0,1]^2),\qquad P_U(E)=\iint_E1\,dt_A\,dt_B\quad(E\in\mathcal F).

The coordinate variables TA(ω)=tAT_A(\omega)=t_A and TB(ω)=tBT_B(\omega)=t_B each have density 1 on [0,1][0,1], zero elsewhere. Independence gives

fTA,TB(tA,tB)={1,(tA,tB)∈[0,1]2,0,otherwise.f_{T_A,T_B}(t_A,t_B)= \begin{cases}1,&(t_A,t_B)\in[0,1]^2,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus PU(E)=Area⁡(E)P_U(E)=\operatorname{Area}(E) for Borel regions in the square. Equal probability per unit area is a property of this measure; it does not follow merely from choosing a square sample space. Individual points have probability zero, so “uniform” means equal-length arrival intervals have equal probabilities, not positive equal probabilities assigned to individual arrival times.

An outcome is one pair; an event is a measurable set of pairs; a random variable maps each pair to a number. All subsequent transformations use this same probability triplet.

A.3. First transformation: the gap between arrival times

Define D(ω)=|TA(ω)−TB(ω)|D(\omega)=|T_A(\omega)-T_B(\omega)|. This is a measurable function with range [0,1][0,1]. It is deterministic given the arrivals, but random before the outcome is known.

A’s arrival (hours) B’s arrival (hours) Gap DD (hours)
0.10 0.20 0.10
0.30 0.80 0.50
0.70 0.70 0
0.90 0.60 0.30

For a real threshold dd, the CDF is FD(d)=PU(D≤d)F_D(d)=P_U(D\le d). When 0≤d≤10\le d\le1,

{D≤d}={|TA−TB|≤d}={−d≤TA−TB≤d}={TA−d≤TB≤TA+d}.\begin{aligned} \{D\le d\} &=\{|T_A-T_B|\le d\}\\ &=\{-d\le T_A-T_B\le d\}\\ &=\{T_A-d\le T_B\le T_A+d\}. \end{aligned}

The inverse image of [0,d][0,d] is the diagonal band between tB=tA−dt_B=t_A-d and tB=tA+dt_B=t_A+d. Each excluded corner triangle has perpendicular sides of length 1−d1-d. Their total area is 2[12(1−d)2]=(1−d)22[\tfrac12(1-d)^2]=(1-d)^2. Therefore

FD(d)=1−(1−d)2=2d−d2(0≤d≤1).F_D(d)=1-(1-d)^2=2d-d^2\qquad(0\le d\le1).

The integration limits give the same result. For the upper excluded triangle, 0≤tA<1−d0\le t_A<1-d and tA+d<tB≤1t_A+d<t_B\le1:

PU(D>d)=2∫01−d∫tA+d11dtBdtA=2∫01−d(1−d−tA)dtA=2[(1−d)tA−tA22]01−d=(1−d)2.\begin{aligned} P_U(D>d) &=2\int_0^{1-d}\int_{t_A+d}^1 1\,dt_B\,dt_A\\ &=2\int_0^{1-d}(1-d-t_A)\,dt_A\\ &=2\left[(1-d)t_A-\frac{t_A^2}{2}\right]_0^{1-d} =(1-d)^2. \end{aligned}

The factor of 2 is justified by the symmetry of this joint law. Include thresholds outside the range to obtain

FD(d)={0,d<0,2d−d2,0≤d≤1,1,d>1.\boxed{F_D(d)=\begin{cases} 0,&d<0,\\ 2d-d^2,&0\le d\le1,\\ 1,&d>1. \end{cases}}

This CDF is absolutely continuous. Its derivative gives a density, up to choices at endpoints:

fD(d)={2(1−d),0<d<1,0,otherwise.f_D(d)=\begin{cases}2(1-d),&0<d<1,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Check normalization: ∫012(1−d)dd=[2d−d2]01=1\int_0^1 2(1-d)\,dd=[2d-d^2]_0^1=1. The gap is not uniform even though both inputs are uniform. Intervals of a given small length near zero receive more gap probability than intervals of that length near one hour. This statement compares intervals, not positive probabilities at individual points.

A CDF always exists for a real random variable. Differentiation recovers the full law as a PDF when the CDF is absolutely continuous; a continuous CDF alone is not a sufficient condition in complete generality.

A.4. Use the gap distribution to answer the meeting question

A 15-minute cap is 1/41/4 hour. The meeting event is M1/4={D≤1/4}M_{1/4}=\{D\le1/4\}. Consequently,

PU(M1/4)=FD(1/4)=2(14)−(14)2=12−116=716=43.75%.\begin{aligned} P_U(M_{1/4})&=F_D(1/4) =2\left(\frac14\right)-\left(\frac14\right)^2\\ &=\frac12-\frac1{16}=\boxed{\frac7{16}=43.75\%}. \end{aligned}

The probability of no meeting is 1−7/16=9/161-7/16=9/16. This uses the distribution of the uncapped gap, not a distribution of actual waiting times.

A.5. Second transformation: cap the wait

The first arrival waits until the second arrives or the allowance expires:

W=min⁡(D,1/4)=min⁡(|TA−TB|,1/4).W=\min(D,1/4)=\min(|T_A-T_B|,1/4).

Equivalently,

W={D,0≤D<1/4,1/4,D≥1/4.W=\begin{cases}D,&0\le D<1/4,\\1/4,&D\ge1/4.\end{cases}

The composition (TA,TB)↦D↦W(T_A,T_B)\mapsto D\mapsto W changes the recorded quantity without changing the original probability measure. The range of WW is [0,1/4][0,1/4].

A.6. Derive the waiting-time CDF in three cases

Start from FW(w)=PU(W≤w)F_W(w)=P_U(W\le w).

Case 1: w<0w<0. A wait cannot be negative, so FW(w)=0F_W(w)=0.

Case 2: 0≤w<1/40\le w<1/4. Because the cap exceeds the threshold,

{min⁡(D,1/4)≤w}={D≤w}.\{\min(D,1/4)\le w\}=\{D\le w\}.

To check both directions: if D≤wD\le w, the minimum is at most ww; if D>wD>w, both DD and 1/41/4 exceed ww, so their minimum also exceeds ww. Hence

FW(w)=FD(w)=2w−w2.F_W(w)=F_D(w)=2w-w^2.

Case 3: w≥1/4w\ge1/4. Every possible wait is at most 1/41/4, so the event is all of Ω\Omega and FW(w)=1F_W(w)=1.

Combining the cases,

FW(w)={0,w<0,2w−w2,0≤w<1/4,1,w≥1/4.\boxed{F_W(w)=\begin{cases} 0,&w<0,\\ 2w-w^2,&0\le w<1/4,\\ 1,&w\ge1/4. \end{cases}}

The middle interval excludes 1/41/4. At that threshold the event suddenly includes all the outcomes whose waits were capped.

A.7. Interpret the jump at 15 minutes

The left-hand limit and the value at the cap are

FW(1/4−)=716,FW(1/4)=1.F_W(1/4^-)=\frac7{16},\qquad F_W(1/4)=1.

For any CDF, a jump at aa has size P(W=a)=FW(a)−FW(a−)P(W=a)=F_W(a)-F_W(a^-). Here,

PU(W=1/4)=1−716=916.\boxed{P_U(W=1/4)=1-\frac7{16}=\frac9{16}.}

The transformation sends the whole set {D≥1/4}\{D\ge1/4\} to the one value 1/41/4. Although PU(D=1/4)=0P_U(D=1/4)=0, the larger event {D≥1/4}\{D\ge1/4\} has probability 9/169/16. A transformation can therefore create a point mass by mapping a positive-probability set onto a single value.

In the visualization, the open point at height 7/167/16 marks the left limit. The filled point at height 1 marks the CDF value at the cap. The vertical connector indicates the jump; it does not mean the function takes intermediate values at w=1/4w=1/4. The CDF is right-continuous.

A.8. Specify the complete mixed distribution

Differentiating below the cap gives the continuous component density

fW,ac(w)=2(1−w)𝟏(0,1/4)(w).f_{W,\mathrm{ac}}(w)=2(1-w)\,\mathbf1_{(0,1/4)}(w).

This is not a normalized PDF for the whole law:

∫01/42(1−w)dw=716.\int_0^{1/4}2(1-w)\,dw=\frac7{16}.

The remaining 9/169/16 is an atom at 1/41/4. For any Borel set B⊆ℝB\subseteq\mathbb R, a complete specification is

PU(W∈B)=∫B∩(0,1/4)2(1−w)dw+916𝟏{1/4∈B}.\boxed{P_U(W\in B)=\int_{B\cap(0,1/4)}2(1-w)\,dw +\frac9{16}\mathbf1_{\{1/4\in B\}}.}

The integral and point mass sum to 7/16+9/16=17/16+9/16=1. Thus the law is mixed, with a continuous component and a discrete component. There is no ordinary density with respect to length that represents all its probability.

One may write the law as fW,ac(w)dw+(9/16)δ1/4(dw)f_{W,\mathrm{ac}}(w)\,dw+(9/16)\delta_{1/4}(dw), where δ1/4\delta_{1/4} is the unit point-mass measure at 1/41/4. This notation does not treat a Dirac mass as a finite-height PDF value. The visualization therefore shows the continuous density and the atom on separate, labelled scales.

Suggested stopping point. Ask students to explain why P(D=1/4)=0P(D=1/4)=0 and P(W=1/4)=9/16P(W=1/4)=9/16 can both hold before calculating moments.

A.9. Mean waiting time: include both components

Expectation is the probability-weighted mean. For the complete mixed law,

E[W]=∫01/4w2(1−w)dw+14916.E[W]=\int_0^{1/4}w\,2(1-w)\,dw+\frac14\frac9{16}.

The integral accounts for waits below 15 minutes; the second term accounts for the full-cap waits. Compute each contribution:

∫01/4w2(1−w)dw=2∫01/4(w−w2)dw=[w2−2w33]01/4=116−196=596,14916=964.\begin{aligned} \int_0^{1/4}w\,2(1-w)\,dw &=2\int_0^{1/4}(w-w^2)\,dw\\ &=\left[w^2-\frac{2w^3}{3}\right]_0^{1/4} =\frac1{16}-\frac1{96}=\frac5{96},\\ \frac14\frac9{16}&=\frac9{64}. \end{aligned}

Therefore

E[W]=596+964=10+27192=37192 hour.E[W]=\frac5{96}+\frac9{64} =\frac{10+27}{192}=\boxed{\frac{37}{192}\text{ hour}}.

With Wm=60WW_m=60W in minutes, linearity gives

E[Wm]=6037192=11.5625 minutes.\boxed{E[W_m]=60\frac{37}{192}=11.5625\text{ minutes}.}

Under independent repetitions of the stated experiment, the sample average converges to this expectation. The result includes both successful and unsuccessful meetings; it is not the conditional mean among pairs who meet. It also differs from E[D]E[D], since a gap above 15 minutes is not fully spent waiting.

A.10. Second moment, variance and standard deviation

The variance is the expected squared deviation from the mean:

Var⁡(W)=E[(W−E[W])2]=E[W2]−(E[W])2.\operatorname{Var}(W)=E[(W-E[W])^2]=E[W^2]-(E[W])^2.

First calculate the second moment using both components:

E[W2]=∫01/4w22(1−w)dw+(14)2916=[2w33−w42]01/4+9256=196−1512+9256=16−3+541536=671536 hours2.\begin{aligned} E[W^2] &=\int_0^{1/4}w^2\,2(1-w)\,dw+\left(\frac14\right)^2\frac9{16}\\ &=\left[\frac{2w^3}{3}-\frac{w^4}{2}\right]_0^{1/4}+\frac9{256}\\ &=\frac1{96}-\frac1{512}+\frac9{256}\\ &=\frac{16-3+54}{1536} =\boxed{\frac{67}{1536}\text{ hours}^2}. \end{aligned}

Now subtract the square of the mean, using a common denominator:

Var⁡(W)=671536−(37192)2=1608−136936864=23936864 hours2≈0.00648329 hours2.\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(W) &=\frac{67}{1536}-\left(\frac{37}{192}\right)^2\\ &=\frac{1608-1369}{36864} =\boxed{\frac{239}{36864}\text{ hours}^2} \approx0.00648329\text{ hours}^2. \end{aligned}

Changing units multiplies variance by the square of the conversion factor:

Var⁡(Wm)=602Var⁡(W)=5975256=23.33984375 minutes2.\operatorname{Var}(W_m)=60^2\operatorname{Var}(W) =\frac{5975}{256}=\boxed{23.33984375\text{ minutes}^2}.

The standard deviation returns to the original units:

SD⁡(Wm)=23.33984375≈4.831 minutes.\operatorname{SD}(W_m)=\sqrt{23.33984375}\approx\boxed{4.831\text{ minutes}}.

The second moment E[W2]E[W^2] and the variance are different quantities. Ignoring the point mass would give incorrect values for both the mean and the second moment.

A.11. Recalculate through survival probabilities

For a nonnegative random variable VV,

E[V]=∫0∞P(V>t)dt.E[V]=\int_0^\infty P(V>t)\,dt.

To see why, write each nonnegative value as its accumulated indicators: V(ω)=∫0∞𝟏{V(ω)>t}dtV(\omega)=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{\{V(\omega)>t\}}dt. Interchange the two nonnegative integrals to obtain the formula. Likewise, V(ω)2=∫0∞2t𝟏{V(ω)>t}dtV(\omega)^2=\int_0^\infty2t\mathbf1_{\{V(\omega)>t\}}dt gives

E[V2]=2∫0∞tP(V>t)dt.E[V^2]=2\int_0^\infty tP(V>t)\,dt.

These identities apply to nonnegative continuous, discrete and mixed laws, with infinite expectations allowed. Here WW is bounded, so the moments are finite.

For 0≤t<1/40\le t<1/4, {W>t}={D>t}\{W>t\}=\{D>t\} and

PU(W>t)=1−FD(t)=(1−t)2.P_U(W>t)=1-F_D(t)=(1-t)^2.

For t≥1/4t\ge1/4, it is zero. Therefore

E[W]=∫01/4(1−t)2dt=[t−t2+t33]01/4=14−116+1192=37192,E[W2]=2∫01/4t(1−t)2dt=[t2−4t33+t42]01/4=116−148+1512=671536.\begin{aligned} E[W]&=\int_0^{1/4}(1-t)^2dt\\ &=\left[t-t^2+\frac{t^3}{3}\right]_0^{1/4} =\frac14-\frac1{16}+\frac1{192}=\frac{37}{192},\\ E[W^2]&=2\int_0^{1/4}t(1-t)^2dt\\ &=\left[t^2-\frac{4t^3}{3}+\frac{t^4}{2}\right]_0^{1/4} =\frac1{16}-\frac1{48}+\frac1{512}=\frac{67}{1536}. \end{aligned}

The survival function falls from a left limit 9/169/16 to 0 at the cap. Its value at that one integration endpoint does not change either integral. No extra atom term is needed in this method: the full-cap outcomes contribute to P(W>t)P(W>t) at every threshold below the cap.
